    <div class="bio">
      <p>I am an ecological macroeconomist. I build data-driven multi-sector models of how the economy, society and the environment interact, and I use them to ask who bears the costs of the ecological transition. My work has appeared in <em>Ecological Economics</em>, the <em>Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization</em>, the <em>Jahrbücher für Nationalökonomie und Statistik</em> and <em>Physical Review E</em>; I co-edited the two volumes of <em>Multiplicity of Time Scales in Complex Systems</em> (Springer/Birkhäuser) and wrote entries for the <em>Dictionary of Ecological Economics</em>; and I have consulted for the World Bank, the Agence Française de Développement and De La Salle University in Manila.</p>
      <p>My research is organised in <a href="/research/">three tracks</a>, each defined by its object and each worked with the same three families of method. <strong>Ecological macroeconomics</strong> asks how open, multi-sector economies stabilise or destabilise under decarbonisation — how prices and business cycles propagate across sectors, and whether states can steer the transition through industrial policy and the management of exhaustible resources while keeping employment and prices stable. <strong>Uneven development and the global structure of production</strong> maps the hierarchy of world production and the flows of labour, value and ecological resources from periphery to core, and asks how the core's decarbonisation is transmitted to the periphery through the international economic order. <strong>An ecological approach to classical political economy</strong> states the classical laws of accumulation and price formation formally enough to be tested, and asks whether the limits to growth are set by nature or by distribution. The methods cut across all three: stock-flow-consistent and multi-regional input–output models; classical and Keynesian dynamical models of multisector growth; and input–output, network and statistical analysis of large data sets, including machine learning. My job market paper, on the macroeconomic consequences of ecological unequal exchange, is where the first two tracks meet. At the Net Zero Industrial Policy Lab I develop the <a href="https://nzipl-cvce.pages.dev/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Clean Value Chain Explorer</a>, a platform that maps the inputs, trade and competitiveness of clean technologies worldwide.</p>

    <div class="section-label">Research Questions</div>
    <ul class="q-list">
      <li>
        <span class="q-dot green"></span>
        Can states steer a multi-sector economy towards sustainability while keeping employment and prices stable, and who bears the cost when the carbon-importing core decarbonises?
      </li>
      <li>
        <span class="q-dot orange"></span>
        Where in the world economy is value produced, and where is it appropriated? How do labour, value and ecological resources flow from periphery to core?
      </li>
      <li>
        <span class="q-dot purple"></span>
        Are the limits to accumulation set by nature or by distribution, and does the labour theory of value give a conservation law of value alongside the irreversibility of accumulation?
      </li>
    </ul>
