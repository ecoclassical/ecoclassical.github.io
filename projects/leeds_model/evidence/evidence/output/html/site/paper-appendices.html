A1 Model equations

In the paper. Cited from the main text (4 reference(s)). Stale passages for item 3 in the rendered text: EU/RoW 2, RoW (alone) 3, OPENFIXG 1, 96 runs 3.

The model consists of difference equations describing the dynamics of the economic variables over a finite time horizon, both at the industry level and at the aggregate level. Each period corresponds to one year. Variables are expressed at time \(t\), unless a “\(- 1\)” subscript is used to denote a lagged value. We use the superscript “\(z\)” to denote each area and, where necessary, “\(f\)” to denote the other area (that is, the foreign sector). In the main application, the two areas are the Global North (\(Z1\)) and the Global South (\(Z2\)), each comprising the 54 industries of the EXIOBASE 3.8.2 aggregation for 2011, so that the matrix of technical coefficients is \(108 \times 108\). Exchange rates are quoted indirectly.20 Industries and products are indexed by \(i\), \(j\) and \(s\), and \(n\) denotes the number of industries in each area. Since the calculations involve scalars, vectors, and matrices, we adopt the following conventions: “\(\odot\)” and “\(\oslash\)” denote Hadamard (element-wise) multiplication and division, while standard matrix and scalar multiplication is denoted by “\(\cdot\)”, and division is expressed using conventional notation. A tilde marks coefficients and shares whose split between domestic and foreign suppliers responds to relative prices, while a superscript “\(0\)” marks the value that a variable takes, at the same date, along the unshocked baseline path of the model. Based on their subject matter, the model equations can be subdivided into fourteen blocks, from Block 0 to Block 13.

Which model this is. Every result in the paper is computed with one configuration of the model, the financial-stress arm with fiat reserve settlement, one row of the registry data/arms.csv. It differs from the submitted model in its price system (prices of production with one uniform rate of profit, replacing 108 calibrated mark-ups), its capital account (sectoral capital–output ratios, a capital-flow matrix for replacement investment, a finance constraint on investment and credit rationing), its household portfolio (a Tobin block with adaptive expectations of returns), its trade block (Armington substitution between origins for final and intermediate goods) and its external closure (fiat reserve settlement at a fixed exchange rate; Block 10 notes the alternative closures of earlier versions).

Reading the reserve equations for North/South. The displayed equations are printed from the configuration in which the central bank of \(Z1\) holds the bills of \(Z2\) (reserve_spec = z1_holds_z2). In the paper’s runs it is the other way round: the South’s central bank (\(Z2\)) holds the North’s bills (\(Z1\)), and the North issues the reserve currency (reserve_spec = z2_holds_z1). The equations are the same with the indices of the two areas exchanged in Blocks 5 and 10 and in the interest and dividend terms of (1.2); the folded engine of the paper’s arm is model/code/arms/MVP_revised_ns_capital_highfinance_openfix_fiat_credit.R. The North/South arm also sets the government’s capital accelerator to \(\gamma_g = 0\), so public investment replaces depreciation.

Where the equations come from. The text of this appendix is the revised model description of 23 October 2026. Each displayed equation is printed from the reported configuration’s rows of qmd/equations/equations.csv, with the OPENFIX-fiat equations of qmd/equations/closure_overrides.csv applied to Block 10, so the displayed algebra is the code’s: it is the algebra of the folded engine that the runs execute. Two equations that the catalogue does not hold separately, the Tobin matrix (7.1a) and the fixed exchange rate (10.0), are printed as the description writes them. Where the description names a parameter more specifically than the catalogue, the displayed equation uses the catalogue’s symbol: \(\psi\) for \(\psi_{fisc}\) (5.2a–5.2b), \(\phi\) for \(\phi_{prem}\) (5.9d) and for \(\phi_{tar}\) (9.1a), \(\gamma\) for \(\gamma_e\) (7.0), \(\varepsilon\) for \(\varepsilon_{fd}\) (9.1d), \(\lambda\) and \(\omega\) for \(\lambda_{land}\) and \(\omega_{wat}\) (11.7, 11.9), \(\rho\) for \(\rho_{f,j}\) (6.8), and \(d_j\) for \(d_{K,j}\). Executed equations that the description does not display are listed at the end of their block.

How one period is solved

Within a period the model is simultaneous: output determines income, income determines demand, and demand determines output. No ordering of the equations is therefore recursive. What exists is the condensation of the period’s dependency graph. Draw an edge from equation \(i\) to equation \(j\) whenever \(j\) reads the current-period value that \(i\) writes. Then contract every strongly connected component — a set of equations each of which depends on every other through a chain of current-period reads — to a single node. The result has no cycles, so it can be ordered topologically: every equation comes after every equation whose current-period output it reads, except inside a component, which is a block of genuinely simultaneous equations. The order was computed from the folded engine (tools/equations/equation_order.R, written to qmd/equations/eval_order.csv) at the level of variable families: the 108 sectoral outputs \(x_j\) count as one family.

The large simultaneous block, S3, holds the circular flow: final demand (2.1), the Leontief solution for output (2.2), value added, income and consumption (2.4, 1.1–1.4), employment and the wage bill (6.1, 6.3–6.4), market and buyer prices (8.3–8.7), import volumes and values (9.1–9.2, 9.4), investment and amortisation (3.2–3.5) and taxes (5.2–5.4). The two small blocks, S1 and S2, are the conversion of material and energy resources into reserves (12.11–12.12 and 12.14–12.15).

The engine solves each period by Gauss–Seidel sweeps over the whole state, with under-relaxation gs_relax = 0.7. A period is accepted when every row’s relative change between two sweeps is below tolerance = 1e-06 and the stock-flow consistency error is below consistency.threshold = 0.01 (model/code/run_model_2026.R:628). A period that has not converged after max.iterations = 1000 sweeps is not accepted. The engine logs the mean relative change, which is of order \(10^{-10}\) at acceptance; the binding criterion is the maximum, \(10^{-6}\). The measured number of sweeps is reported in Appendix B2.

Before the reported run starts, the model runs 60 unshocked periods (the spin-up) from the 2011 data, and the state at the end of the spin-up is the initial state of the reported run (equation 0.1). Scenario interventions start at \(t = 70\).

Block 0. Initial State

The input-output data provide the flows of the base year (2011), but not the stocks: every financial and real stock is initially set to zero. The model is therefore first run without shocks for \(S\) periods, and the first period of every experiment starts from the last state of that initial run:

\[ \mathbf{s}_{t=1} = \mathbf{s}^{\,\text{unshocked}}_{t=S},\quad S = 60 \tag{0.1} \]

At the beginning of the initial run, each government is assumed to have issued a stock of bills to its own central bank. This endowment pins down the level of public debt at which the economy is stationary and is treated as a calibrated parameter. Ecological accumulators start from their base-year values rather than from the initial run. The initial run is solved under the gold-settlement version of the fixed exchange rate regime, whereas the closure described in Block 10 applies from period 1 onwards.

Block 1. Households

Households’ domestic consumption in real terms is:

\[ c^z = \alpha_1^z \frac{YD_w^z}{E(p_A^z)} + \alpha_2^z \frac{YD_c^z}{E(p_A^z)} + \alpha_3^z \frac{V_{-1}^z}{p_{A,-1}^z} \tag{1.1} \]

where \(p_{A}^{z}\) is a consumer price index, while \(\alpha_{1}^{z}\), \(\alpha_{2}^{z}\) and \(\alpha_{3}^{z}\) are the propensities to consume out of disposable labour income (\(YD_{w}^{z}\)), disposable capital income (\(YD_{c}^{z}\)) and net wealth (\(V^{z}\)), respectively.21

Disposable income is net domestic income from firms and banks plus interest received on bank deposits and government bills plus capital gains on holdings of foreign bills and shares minus taxes and interest payments on personal loans:

\[ YD^z = WB^z + DIV^z + FB^z + r_{m,-1}^z M_{h,-1}^z + r_{b,-1}^z B_{s,z,-1}^z + xr^f r_{b,-1}^f B_{s,z,-1}^f + \Delta xr^f (B_{s,z,-1}^f + E_{s,z,-1}^f) - r_{h,-1}^z L_{h,-1}^z - T^z \tag{1.2} \]

where \(WB^{z}\) is the wage bill, \(DIV^{z}\) is distributed profits of firms, \(F_{b}^{z}\) is bank profits (which are assumed to be fully distributed), \(r_{m}^{z}\) is the interest rate paid on bank deposits (\(M_{h}^{z}\)), \(r_{b}^{z}\) is the interest rate on domestic government bills held by domestic households (\(B_{s,z}^{z}\)), \(xr^{f}\) is the nominal exchange rate, \(r_{b}^{f}\) is the interest rate on foreign government bills held by domestic households (\(B_{s,z}^{f}\)), \(E_{s,z}^{f}\) is domestic holdings of foreign shares, \(r_{h}^{z}\) is the interest rate on personal loans granted to domestic households (\(L_{h}^{z}\)), and \(T^{z}\) is income tax payments. Interest on foreign bills is converted at the current exchange rate, that is, at the date on which the issuer pays it (Godley and Lavoie, 2007, ch. 12).

Disposable labour income, disposable capital income and net private wealth accumulated in each area are, respectively:

\[ YD_w^z = WB^z (1 - \theta_w^{eff,z}) \tag{1.3} \]

\[ YD_c^z = YD^z - YD_w^z \tag{1.4} \]

\[ V^z = V_{-1}^z + YD^z - c^z p_A^z \tag{1.5} \]

where \(\theta_{w}^{eff,z}\) is the effective average tax rate on labour income, which equals the statutory average rate, \(\theta_{w}^{z}\), unless the optional fiscal rule (5.2b) is activated. The stock of wealth increases as the household sector saves. Portfolio decisions (that is, the way in which net wealth is held) are discussed in Block 7, while the composition of consumption is discussed in Block 2.

Four indicators of income distribution are also computed, although no other equation depends on them: the profit share and the labour share of disposable income, interest payments by industry (as a residual), and an index of functional inequality, both at the aggregate level and by industry. In formulas:

\[ shp^z\;\;(\texttt{shp}) = YD_c^z / YD^z \tag{1.6} \]

\[ shw^z\;\;(\texttt{shw}) = YD_w^z / YD^z \tag{1.7} \]

\[ int_j^z\;\;(\texttt{int\_j}) = va_j^z - YD_j^z - T_j^z \tag{1.8} \]

\[ ineq^z,\ ineq_j^z\;\;(\texttt{ineq}) = \left| 1 - YD_w^z / YD_c^z \right|,\quad ineq_j^z = \left| 1 - YD_{w,j}^z / YD_{c,j}^z \right| \tag{1.9} \]

Block 2. Production Firms (Current Account)

The final demand addressed to each industry of either area is made up of household consumption, government consumption, private net investment, the replacement of worn-out fixed capital, and government investment, each component being split between domestic and foreign suppliers. Since the model is multi-regional, foreign trade is already embedded in the final demand vector and in the input-output matrix, so that no separate net-export term is required. Considering \(n\) industries and products in each area, the vector of demand for final goods and services is:

\[ \mathbf{d}^z = \tilde{\mathbf{\beta}}^{Z1} c^{Z1} + \tilde{\mathbf{\beta}}^{Z2} c^{Z2} + \tilde{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{Z1} gov^{Z1} + \tilde{\mathbf{\sigma}}^{Z2} gov^{Z2} + \textstyle\sum_z \big[ \tilde{\mathbf{\iota}}^{z} (i_d^{z} - da^{z}) + K_{\cdot, z}\, \mathbf{x}^{z} \big] + \tilde{\mathbf{\iota}}_g^{Z1} i_{d,g}^{Z1} + \tilde{\mathbf{\iota}}_g^{Z2} i_{d,g}^{Z2} \tag{2.1} \]

where \(c^{z}\) is real household consumption, \(gov^{z}\) is real government consumption, \(i_{d}^{z}\) is real private gross investment, \(da^{z}\) is real depreciation, \(i_{d,g}^{z}\) is real government investment, and \({\widetilde{\mathbf{\beta}}}^{z}\), \({\widetilde{\mathbf{\sigma}}}^{z}\), \({\widetilde{\mathbf{\iota}}}^{z}\) and \({\widetilde{\mathbf{\iota}}}_{g}^{z}\) are the vectors of household consumption, government spending, private investment and government investment shares, respectively, whose split between domestic and foreign suppliers responds to relative prices (9.1d)-(9.1e). Net investment (\(i_{d}^{z} - da^{z}\)) is bought according to the investment shares, whereas replacement demand is the bundle of goods actually worn out, by product and by origin, which is given by the empirical capital-flow matrix \(\mathbf{K}\) (8.8).

Once final demands are known, the gross output vector can be defined as:

\[ \mathbf{x}^z = \left( \mathbf{I} - \tilde{\mathbf{A}}^z \right)^{-1} \mathbf{d}^z \tag{2.2} \]

where \(\mathbf{I}\) is the identity matrix and \({\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{z}\) is the matrix of technical coefficients as operated, that is, the technology set by the scenarios, \(\mathbf{A}^{z}\) (13.1), with its split between domestic and foreign suppliers re-weighted by relative prices (13.3). As usual, each element \(a_{ij}\) (with \(i,j = 1,2,\ldots,2n\), since the matrix covers both areas) is the quantity of product \(i\) necessary to produce one unit of product \(j\). Therefore, each column \(j\) is associated with an industry, a technique of production, and a product.

Total final demand and total real gross output are, respectively:

\[ FD^z\;\;(\texttt{fd}) = \textstyle\sum_{j \in z} d_j \tag{2.1a} \]

\[ GO^z\;\;(\texttt{go}) = \textstyle\sum_{j \in z} x_j \tag{2.3a} \]

The monetary value of gross domestic output is the product of the unit price vector and the output vector:

\[ Y^z = \mathbf{p}^{zT} \mathbf{x}^z \tag{2.3} \]

where \(\mathbf{p}^{z}\) is the price vector and the superscript “\(T\)” stands for the transpose of the matrix (hence \(\mathbf{p}^{zT}\) is a row vector).

The net income or value added of each economy matches aggregate nominal demand for final products and services, net of VAT and tariffs:

\[ YN^z = c^z p_A^z + (i_d^z - da^z) p_I^z + da^z p_{rep}^z + i_{d,g}^z p_{I,g}^z + gov^z p_G^z + EXP^z - IMP^z - VAT^z - TAR^z \tag{2.4} \]

where \(p_{I}^{z}\) is an investment price index, \(p_{rep}^{z}\) is the price of the replacement bundle, \(p_{I,g}^{z}\) is the price index of government investment, \(p_{G}^{z}\) is a government spending price index,22 \(EXP^{z}\) is nominal export, \(IMP^{z}\) is nominal import, \(VAT^{z}\) is VAT revenue, and \(TAR^{z}\) is tariff revenue.

Value added by industry is also computed from the production side, at the prices actually received and paid. Exports earn their customs value and inputs are charged at the buyer prices paid by firms (8.13), so that value added summed across industries equals net income:

\[ \mathbf{va}^z\;\;(\texttt{va\_j}) = \mathbf{p}^z \odot \mathbf{x}^z - (\mathbf{p}^z - xr^f\,\mathbf{CV}^{f})\odot \mathbf{m}^{f} - \big(\mathbf{q}_a^{z\top} \tilde{A}\big)^z \odot \mathbf{x}^z \tag{2.4a} \]

\[ VA^z\;\;(\texttt{va}) = \textstyle\sum_{j \in z} va_j^z \tag{2.4b} \]

where \(\mathbf{CV}^{f}\) and \(\mathbf{m}^{f}\) are the customs values (9.1b) and the volumes (9.1c) of the imports of the other area, and \(\mathbf{q}_{a}^{z}\) is the vector of buyer prices paid by firms (8.13). The same quantities valued at the prices of the previous period provide a volume measure of value added and its chained index:

\[ \mathbf{rva}^z\;\;(\texttt{rva\_j}) = \mathbf{p}_{-1}^z \odot \mathbf{x}^z - (\mathbf{p}_{-1}^z - xr_{-1}^f\,\mathbf{CV}_{-1}^{f})\odot \mathbf{m}^{f} - \big(\mathbf{q}_{a,-1}^{z\top} \tilde{A}\big)^z \odot \mathbf{x}^z \tag{2.4c} \]

\[ RVA^z\;\;(\texttt{rva}) = \textstyle\sum_{j \in z} rva_j^z \tag{2.4d} \]

\[ va_g^z\;\;(\texttt{va\_g}) = RVA^z / VA_{-1}^z - 1 \tag{2.4e} \]

\[ va\_index^z = va\_index_{-1}^z\,(1 + va_g^z),\quad va\_index_{1}^z = 1 \tag{2.4f} \]

Total corporate profit in each area is:

\[ FF^z = YN^z - WB^z - r_{l,-1}^z L_{F,-1}^z - AF^z \tag{2.5} \]

where \(r_{l}^{z}\) is the interest rate on loans obtained by production firms (\(L_{F}^{z}\)), and \(AF^{z}\) are amortisation funds.

In addition to using funds for amortisation, production firms retain profits to finance a share, \(\omega_{r}\), of the additional net investment triggered by an intervention, measured as the deviation of lagged nominal net investment from its value along the unshocked baseline path:

\[ FF_u^z = \omega_r\,\frac{p_{I,-1}^z(i_{d,-1}^z-da_{-1}^z)-p_{I,-1}^{z,0}(i_{d,-1}^{z,0}-da_{-1}^{z,0})}{FF_{-1}^z}\,FF^z \tag{2.6} \]

The term is therefore nil in the baseline and before the intervention. A constant retention share would accumulate firm assets without bound, as retained funds would have no use in the stationary state.

Relation to the main text. The replacement purchases \(K\mathbf{x}\) are inside the demand vector (2.1) and iterated on within the period, which gives the same output as the main text’s \(\mathbf{x} = (I - \tilde{A} - K)^{-1}\boldsymbol\Phi\mathbf{f}\).

Block 3. Production Firms (Capital Account)

Firms need fixed capital (in addition to labour and circulating capital inputs) to produce. It is assumed that each industry has its own capital requirement. The target stock of fixed capital, expressed in real terms, also responds to financial conditions:

\[ k^{z*} = \frac{\mathbf{p}_{-1}^{zT}(\mathbf{h}^z\odot\mathbf{x}_{-1}^z)}{p_{I,-1}^z}\left\{1+\beta_m[(\rho-r_l)_{-1}^z-(\rho-r_l)_{-1}^{z,0}]-\beta_l[lev_{-1}^z-lev_{-1}^{z,0}]\right\} \tag{3.1} \]

where \(\mathbf{h}^{z} = \{ h_{j}^{z}\}\) is the column vector of industry-specific target capital to output ratios,23 while \(\beta_{m}\) and \(\beta_{l}\) define the responses of the target to the margin of enterprise and to leverage, respectively. Target ratios are obtained from data as \(h_{j} = d_{K,j}/\delta_{j}\), where \(d_{K,j}\) is the consumption of fixed capital per unit of output and \(\delta_{j}\) is the depreciation rate of the industry. The margin of enterprise is the difference between the realised rate of profit on the capital advanced, \(\rho^{z}\), and the loan rate, while leverage, \(lev^{z}\) (3.6d), is the ratio of loans to the value of the capital stock. Both enter as deviations from the unshocked baseline path, so that the term in curly brackets equals one in the baseline. The realised rate of profit is:

\[ \rho^z\;\;(\texttt{rho}) = \frac{FF^z + r_{l,-1}^z L_{F,-1}^z}{\sum_{j \in z} \big[\mathbf{p}^{T} (\tilde{A} + K)\big]_j x_j} \tag{3.1b} \]

Real gross investment adjusts so as to bridge the gap between the actual capital stock (at the beginning of the period) and its target level, net of the investment that cannot be financed because banks refuse the corresponding loans:

\[ i_d^z = \gamma^z (k^{z*} - k_{-1}^z) + da^z - RAT^z / p_I^z \tag{3.2} \]

where \(\gamma^{z}\) defines the speed of adjustment, \(da^{z}\) is real capital depreciation, and \(RAT^{z}\) is the value of rationed loans (3.6c). Real depreciation is the sum of each industry’s consumption of fixed capital per unit of output, \(d_{K,j}\), times its output:

\[ da^z = \sum_{j \in z} d_j\, x_j \tag{3.3} \]

In this way, there is a single measure of depreciation, which is consistent with the price equation (8.1), since the columns of \(\mathbf{K}\) sum to \(d_{K,j}\). It follows that the real stock of fixed capital in each area is:

\[ k^z = k_{-1}^z + i_d^z - da^z \tag{3.4} \]

Amortisation funds are used to finance the replacement of depleted capital, valued at the price of the replacement bundle:

\[ AF^z = da^z p_{rep}^z - k^z \Delta p_I^z \tag{3.5} \]

The stock of bank loans obtained by production firms is defined as a residual variable:

\[ L_F^z = L_{F,-1}^z + (i_d^z - da^z) p_I^z + da^z p_{rep}^z - AF^z - FF_u^z - \Delta E_s^z \tag{3.6} \]

where \(E_{s}^{z}\) is the nominal value of the stock of shares issued by production firms. Firms have no net worth: loans and shares together finance the capital stock (\(L_{F}^{z} + E_{s}^{z} = p_{I}^{z} \cdot k^{z}\)). Since share issues are driven by households’ demand for equity (3.7), firms’ leverage is ultimately determined by households’ portfolio choices.

Banks may refuse a share of the additional loans that an intervention leads firms to demand (Dafermos et al., 2017). Firms’ demand for new loans is equation (3.6) evaluated at the desired level of investment, \(i_{d}^{D,z} = \gamma^{z} \cdot \left( k^{z*} - k_{- 1}^{z} \right) + da^{z}\), that is, before any refusal:

\[ NL^{D,z}\;\;(\texttt{nl\_d}) = (i_d^{D,z} - da^z) p_I^z + da^z p_{rep}^z - AF^z - FF_u^z - \Delta E_s^z \tag{3.6a} \]

The refused share of new loans increases with leverage and is capped at \(CR_{\max}\):

\[ CR^z\;\;(\texttt{cr}) = \min\!\Big(CR_{max},\ \max\big(0,\ cr_0 + cr_1 (lev_{-1}^z - lev_{ref,-1}^z)\big)\Big) \tag{3.6b} \]

\[ RAT^z\;\;(\texttt{rat}) = \min\!\Big(\max(NL^{D,z},0),\ CR^z \max\big(NL^{D,z} - NL^{D,z}_{ref}, 0\big)\Big)\quad \Big[\text{ceiling: } \min\!\Big(\max(NL^{D,z},0),\ \max\big(0,\ \tfrac{L_{F,-1}^z + NL^{D,z} - L^{*z} p_I^z k^{D,z}}{1 - L^{*z}}\big)\Big),\ L^{*z} = lev_{ref}^z + h\Big] \tag{3.6c} \]

\[ lev^z\;\;(\texttt{lev}) = L_F^z \,/\, (p_I^z\, k^z) \tag{3.6d} \]

where \(cr_{0}\) and \(cr_{1}\) are positive coefficients, and the reference values, \(lev_{ref}^{z}\) and \(NL_{ref}^{D,z}\), are those of the unshocked baseline path at the same date. Banks therefore refuse no loans in the baseline and only ration the additional credit demanded as a result of an intervention. Banks never call loans in, and a scenario that lowers the demand for loans is not rationed at all.

For the sake of simplicity, we assume that share issues are completely demand driven:

\[ E_s^z = E_{h,z}^z + xr_f E_{h,f}^z \tag{3.7} \]

where \(E_{h,z}^{z}\) is the nominal stock of domestic shares held by domestic investors and \(E_{h,f}^{z}\) is the portion held by foreign investors.

The supply of domestic shares to foreign investors, expressed in domestic currency, is therefore:

\[ E_{s,f}^z = xr_f E_{h,f}^z \tag{3.8} \]

The rate of return (in addition to percentage capital gains) on shares issued by production firms of each area is:

\[ r_e^z = \frac{(1-\omega^z) FF^z}{E_s^z} \tag{3.9} \]

where \(\omega^{z}\) is the share of corporate profits that is not distributed to shareholders.

Finally, total dividends (from non-financial firms) received by investors in each area are:

\[ DIV^z = (1-\omega^z) FF^z \frac{E_{h,z,-1}^z}{E_{s,-1}^z} + xr_{-1}^z\, xr^f (1-\omega^f) FF^f \frac{E_{h,z,-1}^f}{E_{s,-1}^f} \tag{3.10} \]

Foreign shares are held in the holder’s currency, so that foreign dividends are converted only once.

Data. In (3.1), \(d_{K,j}\) is the consumption of fixed capital per unit of output (EXIOBASE 3.8.2, 2011) and \(\delta_j\) the Eurostat EU27 2011 depreciation rate of the NACE section of industry \(j\).

Firms’ net worth is not exactly zero. The amortisation funds of (3.5) revalue the end-of-period stock \(k_t\) rather than the stock \(k_{t-1}\) that was held across the price change. Each period the gap \(p_I k - L_F - E_s\) therefore changes by \(-\Delta k_t\,\Delta p_{I,t}\), a second-order term. On the North/South fiat baseline it accumulates to 0.274 (North) and 0.109 (South) at \(t = 100\), which is 4.5 and 4.3 × \(10^{-5}\) of the value of the capital stock, 6,102 and 2,535 (Appendix A3). It is reported, not corrected.

Block 4. Commercial Banks

For the sake of simplicity, it is assumed that commercial banks are always ready to finance firms’ production plans and to fund private investment and consumption expenditures. Supplied loans are, therefore, demand driven:

\[ L_s^z = L_F^z + L_h^z \tag{4.1} \]

Banks provide deposits on demand:

\[ M_s^z = M_h^z \tag{4.2} \]

Banks hold government bills as a fixed share, \(\beta_{b}^{z}\), of their deposits, and borrow from (or deposit at) the central bank to balance their balance sheet:

\[ B_b^z = \beta_b^z M_s^z \tag{4.3} \]

\[ A_d^z = L_s^z + B_b^z - M_s^z \tag{4.4} \]

where \(A_{d}^{z}\) are advances obtained by commercial banks from the central bank. Negative advances represent banks’ deposits (reserves) at the central bank (Godley and Lavoie, 2007, ch. 10).

It is assumed that the interest rate on advances is nil, banks have no costs of production, and there are no compulsory reserves. As a result, bank profits equal the difference between interest received on loans and bills and interest paid on deposits:

\[ F_b^z = r_{l,-1}^z L_{F,-1}^z + r_{h,-1}^z L_{h,-1}^z + r_{b,-1}^z B_{b,-1}^z - r_{m,-1}^z M_{s,-1}^z \tag{4.5} \]

Unlike corporate profits, bank profits are entirely distributed to households.

In the reported arm. Banks supply loans and deposits on demand. They hold the fixed ratio \(\beta_b\) = 0.1 of their deposits in government bills (4.3), and borrow advances from the central bank for any excess of loans and bills over deposits (4.4).

Block 5. Government and Central Bank

Real government consumption grows according to an exogenous rate:

\[ gov^z\;\;(\texttt{g}) = gov_{-1}^z (1 + g_g^z) + gov_0^z \tag{5.1} \]

where \(g_{g}^{z}\) is the growth rate of government consumption and \(gov_{0}^{z}\) is a shock component.

Government investment follows an accelerator towards a target stock of public capital, which is proportional to net income:

\[ i_{d,g}^z\;\;(\texttt{id\_g}) = \gamma_g^z \left(k_g^{z*} - k_{g,-1}^z\right) + da_g^z \tag{5.1a} \]

\[ da_g^z\;\;(\texttt{da\_g}) = \delta_g^z\, k_{g,-1}^z \tag{5.1b} \]

\[ k_g^z\;\;(\texttt{k\_g}) = k_{g,-1}^z + i_{d,g}^z - da_g^z \tag{5.1c} \]

\[ k_g^{z*}\;\;(\texttt{kt\_g}) = \kappa_g^z\, yn_{-1}^z \,/\, p_{I,g,-1}^z \tag{5.1d} \]

where \(\gamma_{g}^{z}\) is the speed of adjustment, \(\delta_{g}^{z}\) is the depreciation rate of public capital, and \(\kappa_{g}^{z}\) is the target ratio of public capital to net income.

Income taxes collected by the government can be calculated using the average tax rates on households’ labour and non-labour incomes. The associated revenue is therefore:

\[ T^z\;\;(\texttt{t}) = \theta_w^{eff,z} WB^z + \theta_c^z (DIV^z + r_{m,-1}^z M_{h,-1}^z + r_{b,-1}^z B_{s,z,-1}^z + xr_{-1}^f r_{b,-1}^f B_{s,z,-1}^f) + T^z_{fisc} \tag{5.2} \]

where \(\theta_{c}^{z}\) is the average tax rate on capital incomes in each area. Two optional debt-stabilising fiscal rules are available, namely a lump-sum tax and a rule on the tax rate on labour income:

\[ T^z_{fisc}\;\;(\texttt{fiscal\_term}) = \psi\,(B_{s,-1}^z - \bar{b}\,VA_{-1}^z) \tag{5.2a} \]

\[ \theta_w^{eff,z}\;\;(\texttt{theta\_w\_eff}) = \theta_w^z + \psi\,(B_{s,-1}^z / VA_{-1}^z - \bar{b}) \tag{5.2b} \]

where \(\bar{b}\) is the target ratio of public debt to value added and \(\psi_{fisc}\) is a reaction coefficient. Both rules are switched off (\(\psi_{fisc} = 0\)) in the baseline experiments.

Households pay VAT on the duty-inclusive price of the goods they buy, while tariffs are levied on the customs value of every imported unit, whether intermediate or final. Government revenues from VAT and tariffs are, respectively:

\[ VAT^z\;\;(\texttt{vat\_rev}) = \sum_s \tau_{vat,s}^z \, \mathrm{CIF}_s^z \, \beta_s^z c^z \tag{5.3} \]

\[ TAR^z\;\;(\texttt{tar\_rev}) = \sum_s \tau_s^z \, CV_s^z \, m_s^z \tag{5.4} \]

where \(\tau_{vat,s}^{z}\) and \(\tau_{s}^{z}\) are the product-specific VAT rates and percentage tariffs, respectively, \(CIF_{s}^{z}\) is the buyer price, excluding VAT (8.13), \(CV_{s}^{z}\) is the customs value of imports (9.1b), and \(m_{s}^{z}\) is the total import of good \(s\) (9.1c).

The government budget deficit in each area is:

\[ DEF_g^z\;\;(\texttt{gdef}) = gov^z p_G^z + r_{b,-1}^z B_{s,-1}^z - F_{cb}^z - T^z - VAT^z - TAR^z + i_{d,g}^z p_{I,g}^z \tag{5.5} \]

where \(F_{cb}^{z}\) is the profit made by the central bank (seigniorage income) on its holdings of (both domestic and foreign) government securities, which is subsequently returned to the government sector.

The government sector issues bills as it runs into deficits. The ratio of public debt to value added is also computed:

\[ B_s^z\;\;(\texttt{b\_s}) = B_{s,-1}^z + DEF_g^z \tag{5.6a} \]

\[ \texttt{debt\_gdp} = B_s^z / VA^z \tag{5.6c} \]

Advances to commercial banks are provided on demand:

\[ A_s^z = A_d^z \tag{5.7a} \]

Similarly, the supply of cash adjusts to the demand for cash:

\[ H_s^z = H_h^z \tag{5.6b} \]

This is the overall amount of state money that remains in circulation at the end of each period.

The stock of bills supplied to domestic investors is:

\[ B_{s,z}^z = B_{h,z}^z \tag{5.7b} \]

whereas the stock of bills supplied to foreign investors is:

\[ B_{s,f}^z = xr^f B_{h,f}^z \tag{5.8} \]

The profit made by the central bank is:

\[ F_cb^z = r_{b,-1}^z B_{cb,z,-1}^z + xr^f r_{b,-1}^f B_{cb,s,z,-1}^f \tag{5.9} \]

where \(B_{cb,z}^{z}\) is the amount of domestic government bills held by the domestic central bank, and \(B_{cb,s,z}^{f}\) is the amount of foreign government bills held by the domestic central bank, expressed in foreign currency.

The policy rate set by the central bank, \(r^{*z}\), is exogenous, except for a risk premium charged on the area whose central bank holds the bills of the other area as reserves (see Block 10). This area pays a premium on the net foreign liabilities that an intervention creates, relative to the unshocked baseline path, expressed as a share of net income:

\[ r^{*z}\;\;(\texttt{r\_star}) = \bar{r}^{*z} + prem^z \tag{5.9b} \]

\[ nfl^z\;\;(\texttt{nfl}) = nfl_{-1}^z - CAB^z \tag{5.9c} \]

\[ prem^z\;\;(\texttt{prem}) = \max\!\left(0,\; \phi\, \frac{nfl_{-1}^z - nfl_{ref,-1}^z}{yn_{-1}^z}\right) \tag{5.9d} \]

where \({\bar{r}}^{*z}\) is the exogenous component of the policy rate, \(nfl^{z}\) is the stock of net foreign liabilities accumulated since the beginning of the main run, \(CAB^{z}\) is the current account balance (9.6), and \(\phi_{prem}\) is a positive coefficient. The premium is nil in the other area and in the baseline. Since it raises the bill rate, it also affects the rate of profit (8.11) and, hence, prices of production.

Finally, interest rates on bank deposits, government bills, loans to firms, and personal loans are defined using different mark-ups (\(\mu_{s}^{z}\)) over the policy rate, that is:

\[ r_m^z = r^{*z} + \mu_m^z \tag{5.10} \]

\[ r_b^z = r^{*z} + \mu_b^z \tag{5.11} \]

\[ r_l^z = r^{*z} + \mu_l^z \tag{5.12} \]

\[ r_h^z = r^{*z} + \mu_h^z \tag{5.13} \]

We assume that, in each area, \(r_{h} \geq r_{l} \geq r_{b} \geq r_{m}\) in the baseline scenario.

In the reported arm. Real government consumption grows at the exogenous rate \(g_g\), which is zero, so it is constant (5.1); government investment closes a fraction \(\gamma_g\) = 0.15 (data/arms.csv) of the gap to a target capital stock (5.1a–5.1d). Interest rates are constant spreads over the policy rate (5.10–5.13). In the reserve-holding region the policy rate carries a premium on the net foreign liabilities that an intervention creates, measured against the unshocked path (5.9b, 5.9d). The budget deficit is financed by bills (5.5–5.8). Central-bank profits, the interest on its bill holdings, are paid to the treasury (5.9).

The fiscal rules (Section 3.3). Real government spending responds to the gap between the region’s net official reserve position and its path in the unshocked run:

\[g^z_t = g^{*z}_t + \theta^z\,\frac{NR^z_{t-1} - \overline{NR}{}^z_{t-1}}{p^z_{g,t-1}}, \tag{5.14}\]

where \(NR^z\) is the value of the gold held by region \(z\)’s central bank plus the foreign bills it holds less the region’s own bills held by the foreign central bank, \(\overline{NR}{}^z\) its value in the unshocked run, \(p^z_g\) the price of government spending, and \(g^{*z}\) the spending path without the rule. \(\theta^z = 0.93\) for the adjusting government and 0 otherwise.

Block 6. Population and the Labour Market

The employment level is determined by firms’ demand for labour in each production process. More precisely, the number of workers hired in each industry is:

\[ N_j^z = \frac{x_j^z}{pr_j^z} \tag{6.1} \]

\(\forall j = 1,2,\ldots,n\), where \(pr_{j}^{z}\) is the output per employee ratio in the \(j\)-th industry.

Total employment in each area is:

\[ N^z = \sum_j N_j^z \tag{6.2} \]

The wage bill paid in each industry is:

\[ WB_j^z = n_j^z w_j^z \tag{6.3} \]

where \(w_{j}^{z}\) is the average money wage rate paid to employees of industry \(j\).

The total wage bill is:

\[ WB^z = \sum_j WB_j^z \tag{6.4} \]

The available labour force in each area’s industries depends on an exogenous growth rate and the net inflow of immigrants from the other area:

\[ POP^z = POP_{-1}^z \odot (\mathbf{1} + g_{pop}^z) + IMM^z - IMM^f \tag{6.5} \]

where \(\mathbf{IMM}^{z}\) and \(\mathbf{IMM}^{f}\) are the vectors defining inflows and outflows of labour force in each area’s industries.

Industry-specific unemployment rates in each area are:

\[ un_j^z = 1 - \frac{N_{j,-1}^z}{POP_{j,-1}^z} \tag{6.6} \]

We assume that immigration inflows depend on three factors: a) the size of the population of the other area; b) the unemployment rate of the other area; c) the wage differential between the two areas. In formal terms, we obtain:

\[ IMM^z = \gamma_{imm,0}^z \odot POP_{-1}^f + \gamma_{imm,1}^z \odot un_{-1}^f + \gamma_{imm,2}^z \odot (w_{-1}^z - w_{-1}^f) \tag{6.7} \]

where \(\mathbf{\gamma}_{imm,0}^{z}\), \(\mathbf{\gamma}_{imm,1}^{z}\) and \(\mathbf{\gamma}_{imm,2}^{z}\) are non-negative coefficients, which are set to zero in the experiments reported.

Gender segregation is assumed to depend on the wage level. Since men tend to occupy high-salary jobs, the percentage of female employees (\(\rho_{f,j}^{z}\)) in each industry falls as the wage rate increases. Female employment by industry and in total is therefore:

\[ \rho_j^z = \rho_{0j}^z - \rho_{1j}^z (w_j^z - w_{j,-1}^z) \tag{6.8} \]

\[ N_{f,j}^z\;\;(\texttt{nf\_j}) = N_j^z\,\rho_j^z \tag{6.2a} \]

\[ N_f^z\;\;(\texttt{nf}) = \textstyle\sum_j N_{f,j}^z \tag{6.2b} \]

where \(\rho_{0j}^{z}\) and \(\rho_{1j}^{z}\) are positive coefficients.

Finally, a uniform rate of profit (Block 8) requires a single money wage in each area. The money wage is anchored at the base-year (2011) employment-weighted average of industry wages:

\[ w_j^z = w_{j,-1}^z \tag{6.9} \]

where \(w_{i}^{z,wb}\) is the base-year wage rate of industry \(i\). Since the weights are fixed, the money wage does not drift with the composition of employment and the price level is determinate.

In the reported arm. Employment is output divided by labour productivity, industry by industry (6.1). The money wage is one rate per region, anchored at its 2011 employment-weighted mean \(\bar w^z\) (6.9a) and constant over the run (6.9), so the wage bill moves with employment and not with a wage-setting rule: there is no Phillips curve in this arm, and the anchored wage pins the price level. Female employment (6.2a–6.2b) is computed for reporting and read by nothing else. The three immigration coefficients of (6.7) are zero in every industry of both regions (rows Z*_gamma_imm_0–_2 of the baseline), so there is no migration.

Block 7. Portfolio Choices

Households hold five assets: domestic government bills (\(B_{h,z}^{z}\)), foreign government bills (\(B_{h,z}^{f}\)), domestic shares (\(E_{h,z}^{z}\)), foreign shares (\(E_{h,z}^{f}\)), and bank deposits. Their demand for the first four, as shares of net wealth, is defined by a Tobinesque portfolio equation:

\[ B_{h,z}^z / V^z = \lambda^{B,0}_{z,z} + \Big[\Lambda_z\,\big(\mathbf{r}^{e,z}_t - \bar{\mathbf{r}}_z\big)\Big]_{B_z},\quad \Lambda_z = \sigma\,\big(\mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{w}_z - \mathbf{w}_z\mathbf{w}_z^{\top}\big) \tag{7.1} \]

\[ B_{h,z}^f / V^z = \lambda^{B,0}_{z,f} + \Big[\Lambda_z\,\big(\mathbf{r}^{e,z}_t - \bar{\mathbf{r}}_z\big)\Big]_{B_f},\quad \Lambda_z = \sigma\,\big(\mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{w}_z - \mathbf{w}_z\mathbf{w}_z^{\top}\big) \tag{7.2} \]

\[ E_{h,z}^z / V^z = \lambda^{E,0}_{z,z} + \Big[\Lambda_z\,\big(\mathbf{r}^{e,z}_t - \bar{\mathbf{r}}_z\big)\Big]_{E_z},\quad \Lambda_z = \sigma\,\big(\mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{w}_z - \mathbf{w}_z\mathbf{w}_z^{\top}\big) \tag{7.3} \]

\[ E_{h,z}^f / V^z = \lambda^{E,0}_{z,f} + \Big[\Lambda_z\,\big(\mathbf{r}^{e,z}_t - \bar{\mathbf{r}}_z\big)\Big]_{E_f},\quad \Lambda_z = \sigma\,\big(\mathrm{diag}\,\mathbf{w}_z - \mathbf{w}_z\mathbf{w}_z^{\top}\big) \tag{7.4} \]

with:

\[ \mathbf{\Lambda}_{z} = \sigma_{p} \cdot \left( diag\left( \mathbf{w}_{p,z} \right) - \mathbf{w}_{p,z} \cdot \mathbf{w}_{p,z}^{T} \right) \tag{7.1a} \]

where \(\lambda_{0}^{B}\) and \(\lambda_{0}^{E}\) are the autonomous shares of bills and shares in households’ wealth, \({\bar{\mathbf{r}}}_{z}\) and \(\mathbf{w}_{p,z}\) are the vectors of rates of return and portfolio shares at the beginning of the experiment (read from the last period of the initial run, Block 0), and \(\sigma_{p} > 0\) is a scale parameter. The product \(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{z} \cdot \left( \mathbf{r}_{t}^{ex,z} - {\bar{\mathbf{r}}}_{z} \right)\) is a column vector with one entry for each of the five assets, and the subscript on each bracket selects the entry of the asset on the left-hand side: for instance, \(\lbrack \cdot \rbrack_{B_{z}}\) is the entry for domestic government bills. Therefore, equation (7.1) reads \(B_{h,z}^{z}/V^{z} = \lambda_{0,z,z}^{B} + \sum_{\mathcal{l}}^{}\Lambda_{z,\left( B_{z}\mathcal{,l} \right)} \cdot \left( r_{\mathcal{l,}t}^{ex,z} - {\bar{r}}_{\mathcal{l,}z} \right)\), where \(\mathcal{l}\) runs over the five assets. There is no equation for deposits, which are the residual asset (7.7). Expected (ex-ante) rates of return are adaptive:

\[ \mathbf{r}^{e,z}_t = \mathbf{r}^{e,z}_{t-1}+\gamma(\mathbf{r}^{z}_{t-1}-\mathbf{r}^{e,z}_{t-1}) \tag{7.0} \]

where \(\mathbf{r}^{z} = \left( r_{b}^{Z1},r_{b}^{Z2},r_{e}^{Z1},r_{e}^{Z2},r_{m}^{z} \right)\) is the vector of realised rates of return on the five assets, the return on each foreign asset including the realised percentage capital gain, \(\Delta xr^{f}/xr^{f}\), and \(\gamma_{e}\) is the share of the gap between realised and expected returns that is closed in each period. Notice that the entries of \(\mathbf{r}^{z}\) are ordered by area rather than by domestic or foreign origin: for the households of the second area, domestic bills (\(B_{z}\)) and domestic shares (\(E_{z}\)) correspond to the second and fourth entries, respectively.

The matrix \(\mathbf{\Lambda}_{z}\) replaces the individual coefficients on rates of return and income of a standard Tobinesque specification. By construction: a) it is symmetric, so that cross-effects are reciprocal; b) its rows and columns sum to zero, so that the five portfolio shares always add up to one; c) its own-return entries are positive and its cross-return entries negative; d) its effect is nil at the initial rates of return, so that the baseline portfolio is the observed one. In other words, the adding-up and symmetry conditions discussed by Godley and Lavoie (2007, sec. 5.6.2 and 5.6.3) are satisfied by construction rather than by calibration.

In each area, households’ demand for cash is proportional to their expected consumption expenditures (proxied by past consumption):

\[ H_h^z = \lambda_c^z c_{-1}^z p_{A,-1}^z \tag{7.5} \]

Households’ demand for personal loans is driven by their purchases of durable goods and by their consumption exceeding disposable income:

\[ L_h^z = L_{h,-1}^z (1 - \psi_1^z) + \max( c^z p_A^z - YD^z, \; \psi_2^z \Delta(p^{zT} dc^z) ) \tag{7.6} \]

where \(\psi_{1}^{z}\) is the share of loans repaid in each period, \(\psi_{2}^{z}\) is the share of consumption funded by bank loans, and \(\mathbf{dc}^{z}\) is the vector defining the real stocks of durable goods.

In each area, bank deposits are the buffer stock of domestic investors:

\[ M_h^z = V^z + L_h^z - H_h^z - B_{h,z}^z - B_{h,z}^f - E_{h,z}^z - E_{h,z}^f \tag{7.7} \]

In the reported arm. to realised returns at speed \(\gamma\) (7.0). The base shares \(\mathbf{w}_z\) and the reference returns \(\bar{\mathbf{r}}_z\) are those of the last spin-up period. The parameters are in Appendix B1 and Appendix A2.

Block 8. Price Setting and Production Function

Firms set prices of production by applying a uniform rate of profit, \(r\), to the circulating and fixed capital advanced. The vector of prices of production is:

\[ p^{z*} = w^z \oslash pr^z + (1+r)\, p^{*} \big[\Xi \odot (\tilde{A} + K)\big] \tag{8.1} \]

where \(\mathbf{\Xi}\) converts the cost of each imported input into the currency of the buying area, at the price paid by its firms (8.14), \(\mathbf{K}\) is the matrix of fixed capital used up per unit of output, and \(\mathbf{p}^{*}\) is the row vector that stacks the prices of production of both areas. Both the rate of profit and the money wage (6.9) are uniform across industries, as in Sraffa (1960), so that we assume a tendency for industry-specific profit rates to level out. The matrix \(\mathbf{K}\) is the empirical capital-flow matrix:

\[ D^z\;\;(\texttt{D.matrix};\ \textsf{revised}:\ \texttt{K.matrix}) = K,\ \ K_{ij} = \bar{K}_{ij} / x_j,\ \ \mathbf{1}^{\top} K = d^{\top},\ \ d = CFC \oslash x \tag{8.8} \]

where \({\bar{K}}_{ij}\) is the consumption of fixed capital of industry \(j\) that consists of product \(i\) (from either origin), and \(d_{K,j}\) is the consumption of fixed capital (\(CFC\)) per unit of output.

Following Sraffa (1960, sec. 44) and Pivetti (1991), the rate of profit is governed by the money rate of interest. More precisely, it equals the value-added-weighted average of the bill rates of the two areas plus a normal profit of enterprise, \(e\):

\[ r\;\;(\texttt{r}) = \frac{\sum_z r_{b,-1}^z VA_{-1}^z}{\sum_z VA_{-1}^z} + e \tag{8.11} \]

The normal profit of enterprise is not a free parameter: it is set at the value at which prices of production reproduce the observed base-year price level at the observed output mix. Since the rate of profit is set by the rate of interest, distribution is an outcome of monetary policy rather than of industry-specific mark-ups. The maximum rate of profit, the standard commodity, the profit share of the standard net product, and the money value of the standard gross product are, respectively:

\[ R\;\;(\texttt{R}) = \frac{1}{\lambda_{\max}(\tilde{A} + K)} - 1 \tag{8.9} \]

\[ x^* = \frac{v_{\max}(\tilde{A} + K)}{\sum_s v_{\max,s}(\tilde{A} + K)} \tag{8.12} \]

\[ \pi\;\;(\texttt{profit\_share}) = \pi_{std} = r / R \tag{8.10} \]

\[ p_{std} = p^{*} \cdot x^{*} \tag{8.12b} \]

where \(\lambda_{\max}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_{\max}\) are the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue and right eigenvector. \(R\) and \(\mathbf{x}^{std}\) are computed on the technique as operated, including its split between origins, but without \(\mathbf{\Xi}\), since the exchange rate and duties concern valuation rather than technique. \(p_{std}\) is used as a measure of the price level, not as a normalisation.

Buyers pay different prices for the same good. Households pay the duty-inclusive import price grossed up by VAT, whereas firms and the government pay it without VAT:

\[ q^z = q_{hh}^z = CIF^z \cdot (1 + \tau_{vat}^z), \qquad q_a^z = CIF^z \tag{8.13} \]

where \(\mathbf{CIF}^{z}\) is the vector of buyer prices, excluding VAT, of the goods purchased in area \(z\) from either origin. For goods produced in area \(z\), these coincide with market prices, \(\mathbf{p}^{z}\) (8.3), whereas for imported goods they are the cost, insurance and freight (CIF) prices, inclusive of the share of duties passed through to the buyer, as defined by equation (9.1a).

An imported input enters the cost of production at the price paid by the firms of the buying area:

\[ \Xi\;\;(\texttt{cost\_conversion}) = \Xi_{sj} = 1\ \ (s, j\ \text{same region}),\qquad \Xi_{sj} = xr^f\,(1 + \phi^z \tau_s^z)\ \ (s \in f,\ j \in z) \tag{8.14} \]

where \(\phi_{tar}^{z}\) is the share of the duty that is passed through to the buyer’s price (9.1a).

In each industry, potential output is simply defined as a direct, linear function of the available labour force:

\[ x^{z*} = pr^z \odot POP^z \tag{8.2} \]

Actual market prices rise above (or fall below) prices of production if actual outputs exceed (or are lower than) potential outputs.24 Market prices are basic prices, that is, they do not include VAT, which is included in the household buyer price (8.13) and in the VAT base (5.3):

\[ p^z = p^{z*} + \Gamma_x^z \odot (x^z - x_{-1}^{z*}) \tag{8.3} \]

where \(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{x}^{z}\) is a vector of positive coefficients defining the sensitivity of market prices to output gaps.

The average prices that convert each component of aggregate demand into real terms are taken over the buyer prices actually paid by the buyer concerned, from either origin: households pay VAT, whereas firms and the government do not. The average price level faced by domestic households depends on the basket of goods they consume in each period:

\[ p_A^z = \big[ \mathrm{CIF}^z \odot (1 + \tau_{vat}^z) \big]^{\top} \beta^z \tag{8.4} \]

Similarly, the average prices paid by production firms to buy investment goods, by the government to buy investment goods, and by production firms to replace worn-out capital are, respectively:

\[ p_I^z = (\mathrm{CIF}^z)^{\top} \iota^z \tag{8.5} \]

\[ p_{I,g}^z\;\;(\texttt{pid\_g}) = (\mathrm{CIF}^z)^{\top} \tilde{\iota}_g^z \tag{8.5a} \]

\[ p_{rep}^z = \frac{(\mathrm{CIF}^z)^{\top} (K x)^z}{\mathbf{1}^{\top} (K x)^z} \tag{8.5b} \]

The average price paid by the government for its consumption is:

\[ p_G^z = (\mathrm{CIF}^z)^{\top} \sigma^z \tag{8.6} \]

Finally, the average price of imports is the ratio of nominal to real total imports:

\[ p_M^z = \frac{NIMP^z}{imp_{tot}^z} \tag{8.7} \]

where \(imp_{tot}^{z} = \sum_{s}^{}m_{s}^{z}\).

Two properties bound what the price block can do. First, distribution is exogenous: \(r\) moves only with the bill rate, which is a constant spread over a constant policy rate except where the net-foreign-liability premium (5.9d) is active. Second, the money wage is anchored, so the price level is pinned. Relative prices move with the technique (\(\tilde A\) re-weights with relative buyer prices; \(K\) is fixed) and with the exchange rate and duties through \(\Xi\).

Distribution and technical change. Three measures of distribution are linked by one identity. Write \(M = \Xi\odot(\tilde A + K)\) for the means of production per unit of output, so that the price of production reads \(\mathbf p = \mathbf w\oslash\mathbf{pr} + (1+r)\,\mathbf pM\). For any gross output vector \(\mathbf x\), the net product at these prices is \(\mathbf p(I - M)\mathbf x\), of which profits are \(r\,\mathbf pM\mathbf x\). The profit share of the net product is therefore the rate of profit times the value of the means of production per unit of net product:

\[\pi = r\,\kappa, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\mathbf pM\mathbf x}{\mathbf p(I - M)\mathbf x}.\]

For Sraffa’s standard commodity \(\mathbf x^{*}\), \(M\mathbf x^{*} = \mathbf x^{*}/(1+R)\), so \(\kappa^{*} = 1/R\) whatever the prices, and the profit share is the standard profit share \(\pi_{std} = r/R\) (8.10). For the output actually produced,

\[\pi = \pi_{std}\,\frac{\kappa}{\kappa^{*}}, \qquad \Delta\ln\pi = \underbrace{\Delta\ln r}_{\text{money rate}} \;-\; \underbrace{\Delta\ln R}_{\text{technique}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Delta\ln(\kappa/\kappa^{*})}_{\text{composition}}.\]

An intervention that changes the technique acts through the second term. With \(r\) set by the bill rate and the money wage anchored, a technique that uses fewer means of production per unit of output raises \(R\), lowers the prices of the goods whose inputs it saves, raises the real wage and lowers \(\pi_{std}\); a substitution towards input-heavier secondary production does the reverse. The realised share adds the third term: the change in the composition of output relative to the standard commodity, which can offset or reverse the technique effect. The share reported in the Results is the regional capitalist share of disposable income, \(shp_z = ydc_z/yd_z\), which follows its region’s realised functional profit share after interest, dividends, retention and taxes. Technical change therefore moves both \(\pi_{std}\) and \(shp_z\), but one for one only if output were in standard proportions and the regions were one; the next paragraph compares them on the reported runs. Distribution does not feed back into prices: neither \(\pi_{std}\) nor \(shp_z\) enters the price equation, and the central bank sets \(r\), not the reported share.

The standard profit share is not the reported capitalist share. \(\pi = r/R\) is a global standard-system ratio; the share reported in the Results, \(shp_z = ydc_z/yd_z\), is region \(z\)’s capitalist share of disposable income, after taxes and interest. The active price equation reads neither. Measured on the twelve interventions under North/South (shock minus own baseline at \(t = 100\); both identities hold to \(10^{-12}\)), the two are only weakly correlated: the correlation of \(\Delta\pi\) with \(\Delta shp\) is 0.68 for the North and 0.42 for the South. What co-movement there is has one cause, not a feedback. An intervention moves the bill rate of the region lower in the monetary hierarchy, the South (the key currency’s rate does not move), and that bill rate enters both \(r\), with a weight equal to the South’s share of world value added (0.33), and interest income, hence \(ydc_z\). What separates the two is a change in the technique operated, which moves \(R\): in sc8 Int-Metal, \(R\) rises by 0.150% and \(r\) by 0.0218 pp, so \(\pi\) falls by 0.026 pp while the South’s \(shp\) rises by 0.046 pp. All of these changes are small: the largest \(|\Delta\pi|\) is 0.040 pp, and the largest \(|\Delta shp|\) is 0.047 (North) and 0.059 (South) pp. The capitalist share can feed into prices only through the optional endogenous_pi branch (model/code/MVP_model_2026.R), which is off in every reported run. A possible extension: technical pass-through to the rate of profit. In the reported closure, technical change cannot move the rate of profit. The rate is set by the money rate of interest plus a constant premium, \(r = r_b^w + e\) (8.11), and the money wage is anchored, so a technique that raises \(R\) lowers the prices of production relative to the wage: the whole gain goes to the real wage and \(\pi_{std} = r/R\) falls. A one-parameter generalisation lets part of the gain pass to the rate of profit through the premium:

\[ e_t = e\left(\frac{R_t}{R_0}\right)^{\theta}, \qquad \Delta\ln\pi_{std} \approx -\left(1 - \theta\,\frac{e}{r}\right)\Delta\ln R . \]

\(R_0\) is the baseline value. At \(\theta = 0\) this is the present closure. At \(\theta = r/e\) (1.10 on the North/South baseline, where \(e/r\) = 0.91) the standard share is invariant to the technique, and the gain is divided between profits and wages in their baseline proportions. Putting \(\theta\) on the premium rather than on \(r\) as a whole keeps the money rate exogenous, as in Pivetti’s closure. In this arm the policy rate does not read \(r\) (the spin-up rate plus the net-foreign-liability premium, 5.9b), so the extension adds no feedback into monetary policy. It would act through the prices of production, the buyer prices and the deflators, and so on firms’ realised profits and on \(shp_z\). \(\theta\) is not identified by the stationary baseline, whose technique does not move. It would have to come from the observed relation between changes in technique and changes in the normal rate of profit, or be scanned. On the reported runs the technique term is itself small (largest \(|\Delta\ln R|\): 14.97 \(\times 10^{-4}\) log points), so the extension matters for the logic of the closure, not for the size of the results reported here.

Block 9. The Balance of Payments

Imports are not defined by an estimated import function. They are derived from the input-output structure, at prices that include duties:

\[ CIF^z = xr^f \cdot p^{f} \cdot (1 + \phi \cdot \tau^z) \tag{9.1a} \]

\[ CV^z = CIF^z / (1 + \tau^z) \tag{9.1b} \]

\[ m_s^z = [\tilde{A}^{z,f} x^z]_s + [K^{f,z} x^z]_s + \beta_s^{z,f} c^z + \iota_s^{z,f} (i_d^z - da^z) + \iota_{g,s}^{z,f} i_{d,g}^z + \sigma_s^{z,f} gov^z \tag{9.1c} \]

where \(\phi_{tar}^{z}\) is the share of the duty passed through to the buyer’s price, \(\mathbf{CIF}^{z}\) is the vector of duty-inclusive prices of imported goods (that is, the import entries of the buyer-price vector in (8.13)), and \(\mathbf{CV}^{z}\) is the vector of customs values. Total imports of each good \(s\) include intermediate inputs, the replacement of fixed capital, and the foreign-origin part of each component of final demand (superscript \(z,f\)).

The split of each final-demand share between origins responds to the change in relative buyer prices since period 1, according to an Armington specification:

\[ \alpha_{o,s}^z = \bar{\alpha}_{o,s}^z \cdot \frac{\left(q_{o,s,-1}^z / q_{o,s,1}^z\right)^{-\epsilon}}{\sum_{o'} \bar{\alpha}_{o',s}^z \left(q_{o',s,-1}^z / q_{o',s,1}^z\right)^{-\epsilon}} \tag{9.1d} \]

\[ \tilde{\beta}_{o,s}^z\;\;(\texttt{armington\_adjust}) = (\beta_{z,s}^z + \beta_{f,s}^z)\,\alpha_{o,s}^z \tag{9.1e} \]

where \(o \in \{ z,f\}\) denotes the origin (domestic or foreign), \(q_{o,s}^{z}\) is the buyer price of good \(s\) from origin \(o\) (8.13), \({\bar{\alpha}}_{o,s}^{arm,z}\) is the base-year share of origin \(o\) in the purchases of good \(s\), \(\epsilon_{fd}\) is the elasticity of substitution between origins for final demand, and \(\beta_{z,s}^{z} + \beta_{f,s}^{z}\) is the total share of good \(s\), from both origins. In the denominator, \(o'\) is a summation index that runs over both origins. The prime distinguishes it from the specific origin, \(o\), in the numerator. The denominator normalises the origin weights so that they sum to one, and the total share of each good is therefore preserved: the split between origins cannot undo the substitution between goods implied by a scenario. The other final-demand shares (\({\widetilde{\mathbf{\sigma}}}^{z}\), \({\widetilde{\mathbf{\iota}}}^{z}\) and \({\widetilde{\mathbf{\iota}}}_{g}^{z}\)) are re-weighted in the same way.

Real final imports are the foreign-origin part of final demand:

\[ imp^z\;\;(\texttt{imp}) = \sum_s \big[ \beta_s^{z,f} c^z + \iota_s^{z,f}(i_d^z-da^z) + [K^{f,z}x^z]_s + \iota_{g,s}^{z,f} i_{d,g}^z + \sigma_s^{z,f} gov^z \big] \tag{9.1} \]

Nominal imports, valued at their customs value and including intermediate imports, are:

\[ IMP^z\;\;(\texttt{nimp}) = \sum_s CV_s^z \, m_s^z \tag{9.2} \]

The volume of final exports to the other area is:

\[ exp^z\;\;(\texttt{rex}) = imp^f \tag{9.3} \]

The volume of total exports to the other area is:

\[ exp_{tot}^z\;\;(\texttt{tot\_rex}) = imp_{tot}^f \tag{9.3a} \]

Nominal export is:

\[ EXP^z\;\;(\texttt{nex}) = xr^f \cdot IMP^f \tag{9.4} \]

The trade balance of each area is:

\[ TB^z\;\;(\texttt{tb}) = EXP^z - IMP^z \tag{9.5} \]

The current account balance is:

\[ CAB^z\;\;(\texttt{cab}) = TB^z + r_{b,-1}^f B_{s,z,-1}^f xr^f - r_{b,-1}^z B_{s,f,-1}^z + r_{b,-1}^f B_{cb,s,z,-1}^f xr^f + xr^f (1-\omega^f) FF^f \frac{E_{s,z,-1}^f}{E_{s,-1}^f} - (1-\omega^z) FF^z \frac{E_{s,f,-1}^z}{E_{s,-1}^z} \tag{9.6} \]

where interest on foreign bills, including the foreign reserves of the central bank, is converted at the current exchange rate, as in (1.2).

The financial account balance, net of official transactions, is:

\[ KABP^z = \Delta B_{s,f}^z - xr^f \Delta B_{s,z}^f + \Delta E_{s,f}^z - xr^f \Delta E_{s,z}^f \tag{9.7} \]

Finally, the net accumulation of financial assets in each area is:

\[ NAFA^z\;\;(\texttt{nafa}) = DEF_g^z + CAB^z \tag{9.8} \]

In the reported arm. The final-demand Armington elasticity is \(\varepsilon\) = 0.626 (9.1d–9.1e) and the intermediate one \(\varepsilon_{int}\) = 1.0 (13.3).

Block 10. Exchange Rate Closure

As mentioned, exchange rates are quoted indirectly, that is, the exchange rate is the price of one unit of domestic currency expressed in foreign currency. Obviously, the exchange rate of the foreign area is the reciprocal of the exchange rate of the domestic area:

\[ xr^f = \frac{1}{xr^z} \tag{10.1} \]

While a floating exchange rate regime can be assumed, in our experiments we consider a fully exogenous exchange rate, to avoid excess endogeneity:

\[ xr^{z} = xr_{- 1}^{z} \tag{10.0} \]

\[ \Delta xr^z\;\;(\texttt{dxr}) = xr^z - xr_{-1}^z \tag{10.1a} \]

Following Godley and Lavoie (2007, sec. 12.4), the holdings of government bills by the central banks are modelled asymmetrically, and interest-bearing bills of the second area are the reserve asset (OPENFIX closure with fiat reserves). In the paper’s North/South runs the roles are exchanged: the North’s bills are the reserve asset and the South’s central bank holds them (see Reading the reserve equations for North/South above). The balance sheet of the central bank of the first area comprises domestic government bills, foreign government bills, advances to commercial banks, and a fixed stock of gold as its assets. On the liability side, cash is the primary component.25 The balance sheet of the central bank of the second area is similar, but it is assumed that it does not hold government bills issued in the first area. The amount of domestic government bills held by the domestic central bank is obtained as an accounting identity from the central bank’s column of the transactions-flow matrix (changes in stocks):

\[ B_{cb,z}^z = B_{cb,z,-1}^z + \Delta H_s^z - \Delta A_s^z - xr^f\,\Delta B_{cb,s,z}^f \tag{10.2} \]

Similarly, the central bank of the other area holds the residual stock of its own government’s bills, as determined by its own balance sheet. Since each central bank starts with a non-zero net worth (its gold endowment), the identity is written in flow form:

\[ B_{cb,f}^f = B_{cb,f,-1}^f + \Delta H_s^f - \Delta A_s^f \tag{10.3} \]

Under the fixed exchange rate regime, the supply of foreign government bills to domestic households is defined as:

\[ B_{s,z}^f = xr^z \cdot B_{h,z}^f \tag{10.4} \]

The supply of government bills of the second area to the central bank of the first area is the residual of the market for the bills of the second area:

\[ B_{cb,s,z}^f = B_s^f - B_{s,z}^f - B_{s,f}^f - B_{cb,f}^f - B_b^f \tag{10.5} \]

Under this regime, gold stocks are not used for settlement and remain at their initial levels:

\[ OR^z = OR_{-1}^z \tag{10.7} \]

\[ OR^f = OR_{-1}^f \tag{10.8} \]

Therefore, the hidden or redundant equation is the one that matches the amount of domestic government bills held by the domestic central bank with the horizontal constraint (in terms of cross-sector holdings of bills) defined by the balance sheet matrix:

\[ B_{cb,z}^z = B_s^z - B_{s,z}^z - B_{s,f}^z - B_b^z \tag{10.6} \]

Since foreign reserves earn the interest rate on the bills of the second area, which accrues to the first area as central-bank profit (5.9), a current account surplus increases reserves and their interest income, which further increases the surplus. Reserves therefore follow a recurrence of the form \(B_{cb,s,z,t + 1}^{f} = \left( 1 + r_{b,t}^{f} \right) \cdot B_{cb,s,z,t}^{f} + \Phi_{t}\), where \(\Phi_{t}\) is the non-interest component of the current account, expressed in foreign currency, and nothing in the model reacts to the stock of reserves. For this reason, the baseline is calibrated to a nil current account, so that reserves remain close to zero and the debt to value added ratios remain on target. A shock that opens a current account imbalance may nevertheless restart the compounding of reserves.

The accounting structure of the model is now complete. However, a few additional model features have been included to allow for a broader range of experiments, which are discussed below.

The reversed hierarchy. The counterfactual of the Results (the South’s money as the reserve) exchanges the roles: the North’s central bank holds the South’s bills, and the South’s bills are the reserve asset. Equations (10.2) to (10.6) then hold with the two areas exchanged.

Gold settlement. In the canonical alternative (Godley and Lavoie, 2007, sec. 12.4), each central bank is the residual holder of its own government’s bills, the reserve-holding central bank’s stock of foreign bills is frozen, and the balance of payments settles in gold. Gold earns no interest, so the interest circuit described above is absent. On the earlier grid of 96 runs it gave nearly identical responses (Section 3 of the paper, footnote). The engine also implements a fiscal-adjustment closure (OPENFIXG) and a crawling exchange rate; neither is used in the paper, and their equations are in the closure tables of the evidence site.

Block 11. Waste, Emissions

The general form. Every ecological quantity in the model is an intensity times sectoral output. With \(\mathbf u^{k}\) the vector of direct intensities of impact \(k\) (CO2 emissions, material throughput, land use or water use), the sectoral impacts and their regional total are

\[\mathbf q^{k} = \hat{\mathbf u}^{k}\mathbf x, \qquad Q^{k} = \mathbf u^{k\top}\mathbf x = \mathbf u^{k\top}L\,\mathbf d^{\mathrm{nr}} = \mathbf m^{k\top}\mathbf d^{\mathrm{nr}}, \qquad \mathbf m^{k} = L^{\top}\mathbf u^{k}, \tag{11.0}\]

of which (11.3)–(11.4), (11.7)–(11.10) and (12.1) are the particular cases. \(m^{k}_j\), the total intensity, is the impact embodied over every round of production, in both regions, in one unit of final demand for sector \(j\), including the fixed capital that production uses up.

In each area, waste accumulates as goods and services are produced, net of what is absorbed by the waste treatment industries. The waste associated with each domestic industry is calculated using the related waste to output ratio, \(\zeta_{j}^{z}\), that is:

\[ wa_j^z = wa_{j,-1}^z + x_j^z \cdot \zeta_j^z - x_j^z \sum_{r \in W} \tilde{A}_{r,j} \tag{11.1} \]

where \(W\) is the set of the five waste treatment industries. Total domestic waste is therefore:

\[ wa^z = \sum_j wa_j^z \tag{11.2} \]

If one assumes away land emissions, annual emissions of \(CO_{2}\) can be calculated for each industry by multiplying their respective output by the industry-specific energy intensity coefficient:

\[ emis_j^z = x_j^z \cdot \varepsilon_j^z \tag{11.3} \]

\[ emis^z = \sum_j emis_j^z \tag{11.4} \]

In each area, cumulative \(CO_{2}\) emissions are:

\[ co_2^z = co_{2,-1}^z + emis^z \tag{11.5} \]

Atmospheric temperature is simply calculated as a function of \(CO_{2}\) concentration at the global level:

\[ temp = \frac{1}{1 - fnc} \cdot tcre \cdot \left( co_2^z + co_2^f \right) \tag{11.6} \]

where \(fnc\) is the non-\(CO_{2}\) fraction of total anthropogenic forcing, and \(tcre\) is the transient climate response to cumulative carbon emissions.

The usage of land and water is calculated in a similar way:

\[ land_j^z = x_j^z \cdot \lambda_j^z \tag{11.7} \]

\[ land^z = \sum_j land_j^z \tag{11.8} \]

\[ water_j^z = x_j^z \cdot \omega_j^z \tag{11.9} \]

\[ water^z = \sum_j water_j^z \tag{11.10} \]

where \(\lambda_{land,j}^{z}\) is the land intensity coefficient by industry and \(\omega_{wat,j}^{z}\) is the water intensity coefficient by industry.

In the reported arm. Every ecological flow is an intensity vector times sectoral output. Nothing in this block is read back by the economic blocks (Appendix B2).

Block 12. Matter Extraction and Energy Use

In each area, the material contents of outputs can be defined using the corresponding vector of industry-specific matter-intensity coefficients, \(\mathbf{\phi}^{z}\), that is:

\[ x_{mat}^z = \mathbf{\phi}^{zT} \cdot x^z \tag{12.1} \]

The quantity of matter extracted in each period also depends on recycling:

\[ mat^z = x_{mat}^z - rec^z \tag{12.2} \]

Recycled matter includes the recycled part of the discarded socioeconomic stock and the material content of the output of the secondary-material industries:

\[ rec^z = \rho_{dis}^z \cdot dis^z + \sum_{j \in \{12,14,18,22,25,27,30,37\}} \mu_{mat,j}^z \cdot x_j^z \tag{12.3} \]

where \(dis^{z}\) is the discarded socioeconomic stock, \(\rho_{dis}^{z}\) is the associated rate of recycling, \(S_{\sec}\) is the set of the eight secondary-material industries, and \(\mu_{mat,j}^{z}\) is the material content per unit of their output.

The discarded socioeconomic stock is:

\[ dis^z = \mathbf{\phi}^{zT} \left( \zeta_{dc,-1}^z \odot dc_{-1}^z \right) \tag{12.4} \]

where \(\mathbf{\zeta}_{dc}^{z}\) is the vector of the percentages of durable consumption goods discarded every year by product/industry.

New durable goods equal all purchased goods minus discarded goods:

\[ \Delta dc^z = \beta^z \cdot c^z - \zeta_{dc,-1}^z \odot dc_{-1}^z \tag{12.5} \]

Like material contents, the energy contents of outputs can be defined using the corresponding vector of industry-specific intensity coefficients, \(\mathbf{\varepsilon}^{z}\), that is:

\[ en^z = \mathbf{\varepsilon}^{zT} \cdot x^z \tag{12.7} \]

Renewable energy is just a share of total energy used in each industry:

\[ en_R^z = x^{zT} \left( \mathbf{\varepsilon}^z \odot \mathbf{\eta}_{en}^z \right) \tag{12.8} \]

Non-renewable energy is therefore:

\[ en_N^z = en^z - en_R^z \tag{12.9} \]

We can now calculate the global stocks of matter and energy. The annual change in the stock of material reserves is:

\[ \Delta k_{mat} = conv_{mat} - mat^z - mat^f \tag{12.10} \]

Material resources converted into reserves are:

\[ conv_{mat} = \sigma_{mat} \cdot res_{mat} \tag{12.11} \]

where \(\sigma_{mat}\) is the speed of conversion and \(res_{mat}\) is the quantity of resources, which fall as more resources are converted into reserves:

\[ res_{mat} = res_{mat,-1} - conv_{mat} \tag{12.12} \]

Similarly, the equations defining energy depletion are:

\[ \Delta k_{en} = conv_{en} - en_N^z - en_N^f \tag{12.13} \]

\[ conv_{en} = \sigma_{en} \cdot res_{en} \tag{12.14} \]

\[ res_{en} = res_{en,-1} - conv_{en} \tag{12.15} \]

where \(\sigma_{en}\) is the speed of conversion of energy resources into reserves.

Block 13. Circular Economy Innovations

The introduction of a simple CE innovation in the domestic economy entails a reduction in the quantities of products and services used as inputs in the same industries. This is possible because, for instance, recycled inputs now enter their production processes.26 In addition, outputs from other industries are used as inputs in the waste recycling industry.

This model assumes that technical change (that is, the value of CE-associated technical coefficients) is set by policy makers, while the average speed of convergence of technical coefficients to their target values is defined as a linear, positive function of government expenditures. Focusing on the domestic economy, each coefficient is defined as:

\[ a_{ij}^z = a_{ij,-1}^z + \gamma_{A,-1}^z \cdot ce^z \cdot \left( {a'}_{ij}^z - a_{ij,-1}^z \right) \tag{13.1} \]

where \(a_{ij}^{'z}\) is the target coefficient, \(ce^{z}\) is a dummy variable that switches the innovation on at the beginning of the scenario, and \(\gamma_{A}^{z}\) is the average speed of transition towards a (partial) CE production system. The latter is defined as:

\[ \gamma_A^z = \gamma_{A0}^z + \mathbf{\Gamma}_A^{zT} \cdot \sigma^z \cdot gov_{-1}^z \tag{13.2} \]

where \(\gamma_{A0}^{z}\) is a positive scalar, whereas \(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{A}^{z} = \{\gamma_{Aj}^{z}\}\) is the vector that defines the industry-specific sensitivities (of the speeds of adjustment) to government final demands.

The coefficients \(a_{ij}^{z}\) define the technology, that is, the total input of each good per unit of output. How that input is split between domestic and foreign suppliers responds to relative prices, as for final demand:

\[ \tilde{a}_{o,sj}^z\;\;(\texttt{A\_arm}) = \big(a_{z,sj} + a_{f,sj}\big)\,\frac{a_{o,sj}\,\big(q^z_{a,o,s,-1}/q^z_{a,o,s,1}\big)^{-\varepsilon_{int}}}{\sum_{o'} a_{o',sj}\,\big(q^z_{a,o',s,-1}/q^z_{a,o',s,1}\big)^{-\varepsilon_{int}}},\qquad o \in \{z, f\},\ j \in z \tag{13.3} \]

where \(\epsilon_{int}\) is the elasticity of substitution between origins for intermediate inputs and, as in (9.1d), \(o'\) is a summation index running over both origins. For each good and each using industry, the total coefficient, \(a_{z,sj} + a_{f,sj}\), is preserved exactly, and only its origin changes. The resulting matrix, \(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\), is the one used by every equation that reads technical coefficients, namely (2.2), (2.4a), (3.1b), (8.1), (8.9), (8.12), (9.1c) and (11.1).

In the reported arm. An intermediate intervention defines target coefficients \(a'\) for the North: the primary sector’s row is scaled by \(1-\rho\) and the matching secondary sector’s row is raised by \(\rho\,a_{r1,sj}\), so that \(a'_{r1,sj} = (1-\rho)\,a_{r1,sj}\) and \(a'_{r2,sj} = a_{r2,sj} + \rho\,a_{r1,sj}\), with \(\rho = 0.2\) (model/code/production_scenarios_2026.R). From \(t = 70\) the North’s coefficients close the share \(\gamma_A\) of the remaining gap to \(a'\) each period (13.1); \(\gamma_A\) = 0.118 on the reference arm (row Z1_gamma_A of shock_10_run.RDS). A final-demand intervention shifts the share \(\rho\) of the corresponding entry of a final-demand share vector (model/code/demand_scenarios_2026.R).

The intervention operators (Section 3.1).

For each material pair, let sector 1 denote the primary source and sector 2 the secondary substitute, with \(\rho \in [0,1]\).

Final-demand shocks (sc1–sc4) shift fraction \(\rho\) of the primary-material share in a given channel \(\phi^z \in \{\boldsymbol{\beta}^z, \boldsymbol{\sigma}^z, \boldsymbol{\iota}^z\}\) toward the secondary sector:

\[\phi'_1 = (1-\rho)\,\phi_1, \qquad \phi'_2 = \phi_2 + \rho\,\phi_1, \qquad \phi'_j = \phi_j \quad (j\neq 1,2) \tag{13.4}\]

Total expenditure is preserved: \(\sum_j \phi'_j = \sum_j \phi_j = 1\). The resulting demand shock vector has only two non-zero elements:

\[\Delta d_1 = -\rho\,\phi_1\,f^z, \qquad \Delta d_2 = +\rho\,\phi_1\,f^z \tag{13.5}\]

where \(f^z \in \{c^z, g^z, i_d^z\}\) is the regional expenditure aggregate. Output responds via \(\Delta\mathbf{x} = L\,\Delta\mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{nr}}\).

Intermediate-demand shocks (sc5–sc12) modify the A-matrix for all using sectors \(j\) in region \(z\):

\[a'_{1j} = (1-\rho)\,a_{1j}, \qquad a'_{2j} = a_{2j} + \rho\,a_{1j}, \qquad a'_{ij} = a_{ij} \quad (i\neq 1,2) \tag{13.6}\]

Total material input requirements are preserved: \(a'_{1j}+a'_{2j} = a_{1j}+a_{2j}\). For small \(\rho\), the first-order output change is \(\Delta\mathbf{x} \approx L\,\Delta A\,\mathbf{x}\).

Their first-order effect (Section 3.2).

Let \(\mathbf{u}^{k}\) be the intensity vector of ecological impact \(k\) of Section 2.5 — material throughput \(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{mat}}\) (entries \(\mu_j\)), CO2 emissions \(\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{CO_2}}\), land or water — so that the level of the impact is \(Q^{k} = \mathbf{u}^{k\top}\mathbf{x}\). Ceteris paribus, the first-order change in \(\mathbf{x} = (\tilde A + K)\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{nr}}\) is \(\Delta\mathbf{x} \simeq L(\Delta\mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{nr}} + \Delta A\,\mathbf{x})\), with \(L\) the inverse of (2.2) of the paper, and

\[\Delta Q^{k} \simeq \mathbf{u}^{k\top}L\,\bigl(\Delta\mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{nr}} + \Delta A\,\mathbf{x}\bigr). \tag{13.7}\]

For a final-demand shock, (13.5) gives \(\Delta\mathbf{d}^{\mathrm{nr}} = \rho\,\omega\,(\mathbf{e}_2 - \mathbf{e}_1)\), where \(\mathbf{e}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2\) are the unit vectors of the source and substitute sectors and \(\omega = \phi_1 f^z\), so that

\[\Delta Q^{k} \simeq \rho\,\omega\,(m^{k}_2 - m^{k}_1). \tag{13.8}\]

For an intermediate-demand shock, (13.6) gives \(\Delta A = \rho\,(\mathbf{e}_2 - \mathbf{e}_1)\,\mathbf{a}_{1\bullet}^{\top}\) over the set \(J\) of using sectors in region \(z\), so that \(\Delta A\,\mathbf{x} = \rho\,(\mathbf{e}_2 - \mathbf{e}_1)\sum_{j\in J} a_{1j}x_j\) and

\[\Delta Q^{k} \simeq \rho\,(m^{k}_2 - m^{k}_1)\sum_{j\in J} a_{1j}\,x_j. \tag{13.9}\]

A2 The household portfolio block as run

In the paper. Cited from the main text (2 reference(s)). No EU/RoW, RoW, OPENFIXG or 96-run passage.

The household asset demands are a Tobin–Godley–Lavoie portfolio (Appendix A1, Block 7). Each region’s households hold own-region bills, other-region bills, own-region equity, other-region equity, cash and deposits. The four non-deposit shares of wealth are

\[ \frac{A_{z,k}}{V^z} = \lambda^{0}_{z,k} + \Big[\Lambda_z\,(\mathbf{r}^{e,z} - \bar{\mathbf{r}}_z)\Big]_k, \qquad \Lambda_z = \sigma\,(\operatorname{diag}\mathbf{w}_z - \mathbf{w}_z\mathbf{w}_z^\top), \]

cash is \(\lambda_c\) = 0.1 of lagged nominal consumption, and deposits are the residual. \(\Lambda_z\) is symmetric and its rows and columns sum to zero. Tobin’s adding-up, symmetry and gross-substitution conditions therefore hold by construction: the shares add to one, own-return effects are positive and cross-return effects negative. The returns are ordered as own bills, other bills, own equity, other equity and deposits; foreign assets carry the realised currency gain \(\Delta xr/xr\), which is zero under a fixed exchange rate.

Parameters as run. The settings in this arm are:

  • \(\sigma\) = 4;
  • expectations are adaptive, \(\mathbf{r}^{e}_t = \mathbf{r}^{e}_{t-1} + \gamma(\mathbf{r}_{t-1} - \mathbf{r}^{e}_{t-1})\) with \(\gamma\) = 0.1 (data/arms.csv, columns portfolio_sigma, portfolio_expect and portfolio_gamma);
  • the base shares \(\mathbf{w}_z\) and reference returns \(\bar{\mathbf{r}}_z\) are read from the last spin-up period.

B1 Calibration, parameters and initial conditions

Data and what is taken from it directly

The model is built on EXIOBASE 3.8.2 for the observation year 2011. The 2018 date of the database citation is its release, not the year observed. The 44 countries and five rest-of-world blocks are aggregated to two regions, the Global North and the Global South, and the 163 industries to 54 per region (Appendix C1). An EU/RoW partition of the same table was used in earlier versions of the paper.

The following are taken from the data without fitting:

  • the inter-industry coefficients \(A\);
  • the capital-flow matrix \(K\) (EXIOBASE 3.8.2 capital-use matrix for 2011 (Södersten et al., 2018; Wood and Södersten, 2021));
  • consumption of fixed capital per unit of output \(d_j\);
  • labour productivity, employment and its female share;
  • the final-demand share vectors;
  • the emission, material, land and water intensities;
  • the 2011 macroeconomic aggregates used as targets.

The sectoral capital–output ratios are \(\kappa_j = d_j/\delta_{s(j)}\), where \(\delta_{s(j)}\) is the Eurostat EU27 2011 depreciation rate of each NACE section. The price block is calibrated, not fitted:

  • the money wage is anchored at its 2011 employment-weighted mean;
  • the enterprise premium \(e\) is the value at which prices of production reproduce the observed price level (Appendix A1, Block 8).

What is fitted, and to what

Every stock is zero in the workbook. The model is therefore run for 60 unshocked periods (the spin-up), and the reported run restarts from the state the spin-up reaches. The remaining free parameters were set by joint searches over a small number of named instruments, each run to \(t = 100\):

  • the initial government-debt endowments;
  • the intercepts \(\lambda^{E,0}\) of the equity shares of household wealth;
  • scale factors on the two tax rates.

The searches had three objectives: that the baseline be stationary, that the 2011 aggregates be reproduced at \(t = 100\), and that the debt and external positions hit their targets. The fit criterion is the sum of squared deviations of model from target, each measured in units of the region’s value-added target (calib_fitness() in R/calibration/calibration_2026.R). The searches are logged in the workbook’s calibration_search sheet and in output/calibration/. The evolutionary random search described in the previous version calibrated the submitted model and is no longer used.

How a search proceeds. Each search is a Newton iteration with a finite-difference Jacobian, run by hand one round at a time (R/calibration/calibrate_revised_debt.R, R/calibration/calibrate_capital_arm.R). A round consists of the following runs, each a full spin-up and 100-period run executed in parallel:

  1. the current point \(\theta\);
  2. for each instrument \(k\), a run with \(\theta_k\) moved alone by a step \(h_k\). The logged steps are 100 to 500 for a debt endowment, 0.05 for an own-equity intercept, 0.0002 to 0.01 for a cross-border equity share, and 0.004 to 0.02 for a tax scale.

The differences give the Jacobian \(J_{ik} \approx [y_i(\theta + h_k e_k) - y_i(\theta)]/h_k\) of the target measures \(y\) at \(t = 100\). The next point is the Newton step \(\theta - J^{-1}[y(\theta) - y^\ast]\), or a least-squares step when there are more targets than instruments. One measured property makes this cheap. The debt endowment moves the stationary debt level one for one and leaves every flow unchanged to four digits. Its column of \(J\) is therefore known, and it enters linearly.

Method

The folded engine of the fiat-reference arm was parsed with R’s own parser (R/dynamics/dependency_graph.R). The abstract syntax tree of every assignment was walked, and an edge \(j \to i\) recorded whenever the equation of variable \(i\) reads variable \(j\). Two graphs result: \(G_c\), for reads of the current period’s values, and \(G_\ell\), for reads of the previous period’s. Dataflow through local variables is tracked, both branches of every conditional are taken, and the loops over regions are unrolled. The walk resolved all 256 assignments.

\(G_c\) contains cycles, and must: output determines income, income determines demand, demand determines output. The device that separates the cyclic part from the rest is the strongly connected component (SCC), a maximal set of variables in which every member can reach every other by following edges. A variable alone in an SCC of size one takes part in no cycle. A large SCC is an irreducible simultaneous block: no subset of it can be solved before the rest. Contracting each SCC to a single node gives the condensation, which is acyclic and so has a topological order.

This is the graph-theoretic form of Sraffa’s separation of basic from non-basic commodities. A basic enters, directly or indirectly, into the production of every commodity including itself; a non-basic does not, and can be deleted without disturbing the determination of anything else. The basics are the members of the dominant SCC; the non-basics hang off it as an acyclic tail. Here the decomposition is applied to the full equation block — prices, incomes, demand, emissions and inequality together — rather than to the technology matrix alone.

The parse is coefficient-blind: it records an edge whether the multiplier is 0.3 or 0, and it records an elementwise product such as \(va_j = x_j p_j\) as if all 108 sectors entered all 108 equations. The largest SCC obtained from it is therefore an upper bound on the simultaneous core. A variable is called live if it is not constant over the whole baseline run: 2,976 of the 6,747 rows of the state are live, the rest being parameters stored in the same array.

Result

The largest SCC of \(G_c\) has 1,024 variables. It consists of 9 sectoral families at 108 variables each — gross output \(\mathbf{x}\), prices \(\mathbf{p}\), demand \(\mathbf{d}\), employment \(\mathbf{n}_j\), value added \(\mathbf{va}_j\), the wage bill \(\mathbf{wb}_j\), and the three income blocks \(\mathbf{yd}_j\), \(\mathbf{ydc}_j\), \(\mathbf{ydw}_j\) — and 26 aggregate flows and prices at 2 each: \(972 + 52 = 1,024\). The aggregates are consumption, investment, depreciation and amortisation funds, the capital stock, dividends, imports and exports, the deflators, taxes and duty and VAT revenue, value added, net income and disposable income (M_TOT_int, af, c, da, div, div_h1, id, imp, k, nex, nimp, pa, pg, pid, pid_g, pim, t, tar_rev, tot_imp, va, vat_rev, wb, yd, ydc, ydw, yn). That is the circular flow of the classical surplus approach, and nothing else is in it. At family level the same component is the simultaneous block S3 of Appendix A1.

Contracting to variable blocks gives 123 blocks in 86 components, and the condensation is acyclic (checked with igraph::is_dag()). The core is a single node of 35 blocks. Everything to its right is reachable from it, and nothing on that side returns within the period. The layers are longest-path depth from the core: 42 blocks at depth 1, 22 blocks at depth 2, 8 blocks at depth 3, 4 blocks at depth 4. The ecological cascade runs the full width: sectoral emissions at depth 1, aggregate emissions at depth 2, cumulative CO\(_2\) at depth 3 and temperature at depth 4, where it ends. 3 blocks feed the core without receiving from it: p_t, kt, f_b. They are the basic prices of production \(\mathbf{p}_t\), the two capital targets, government investment and its depreciation, and bank profits.

Do the indicators feed back over time?

The condensation answers the question within a period. Over time, an indicator could still feed back through a lagged read. To test this, the current-period and lagged graphs were joined, and for each of 24 indicators it was asked whether any path, of any length, leads back to the core. Only 1 indicator does: gdef. The government deficit adds to the bill stock, and next period’s interest on the bills enters disposable income and taxes.

None of the ecological indicators returns, and none of the distributional ones does either: emissions, cumulative CO\(_2\), temperature, material extraction and recycling, land, water, waste, the material and energy stocks, the wage and profit shares, inequality and unemployment.

What this implies for the model’s scope

In Sraffa’s terms, the basics of this model are the 1,024 variables of the circular flow. Emissions, materials, land, water, inequality and temperature are non-basic in exactly his sense: they can be deleted without disturbing the determination of anything else, in any period. Stated plainly, and without presenting it as a virtue: emissions and material depletion do not feed back into production or prices in this model. Ecological damage is accounted, not transmitted. That is a deliberate simplification appropriate to the question the model is asked — how a circular-economy transition redistributes material throughput and income between core and periphery. But it means the model cannot represent ecological damage constraining accumulation, and results should not be read as if it could.

B3 The model as a dynamical system

In the paper. Cited from the main text (2 reference(s)). Stale passages for item 3 in the rendered text: EU/RoW 2, RoW (alone) 2.

The numerical results in this appendix come from the archived output/scenarios/full_mrio_baseline_2026.RDS and the September 9 transition-matrix experiment, before the current revised arm was built. They describe that historical configuration only. The accounting correction in qmd/reports/2026-09-11-fiscal-and-external-accounts.qmd also changes the interpretation of its two unstable roots. The last subsection reports the transition matrices of the model the Results use, which supersede these numbers.

The model is written as a period loop containing a fixed-point iteration, not as a stated law of motion. It nevertheless has one, and identifying it makes the model’s dynamic properties available to standard analysis.

Writing \(s_t \in \mathbb{R}^{6713}\) for the full state at period \(t\) and \(\theta\) for the parameters, the equation block is

\[s_t = F(s_t,\, s_{t-1};\, \theta) \tag{A5.1}\]

— implicit, because \(s_t\) appears on both sides, which is what the Gauss–Seidel iteration resolves. Whether the system is first-order is an empirical question about the source, and it was checked by counting: of the lag references in the equation block, all 165 are exactly one period (\(t-1\)), and none is deeper; 169 assignments target the current column and none targets a past one. No companion-form augmentation is therefore needed — the state vector as it stands is already the right state for a first-order system.

Linearising (A5.1) about a point on the baseline path, and writing \(J_c = \partial F/\partial s_t\) for the within-period Jacobian and \(J_\ell = \partial F/\partial s_{t-1}\) for the lagged one,

\[ds_t = J_c\, ds_t + J_\ell\, ds_{t-1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad ds_t = \underbrace{(I - J_c)^{-1} J_\ell}_{M}\, ds_{t-1}. \tag{A5.2}\]

The structure of \(M\) is worth naming. \((I - J_c)^{-1}\) is a Leontief inverse — not by analogy but literally the same algebraic object as the input–output inverse \((I - \mathbf{A})^{-1}\), taken across equations rather than across sectors. All of the simultaneity and all of the multiplier structure live in that inverse and contribute no dynamics of their own; it is an instantaneous propagator. Everything genuinely dynamic is in \(J_\ell\).

\(J_\ell\) is extremely sparse in its columns. Of the 2,968 live variables, only 588 are ever read at the previous period, so all other columns of \(M\) are exactly zero: the dynamics are carried by a 588-dimensional state, not a 6,713-dimensional one. The 588 are four sectoral blocks — desired consumption, prices, gross output and target output at 108 each, and sectoral wages at 106 — plus some fifty aggregate stocks (government bills held by banks, the central bank and households; equities issued and held; net wealth; the capital stock; loans; deposits; cumulative CO\(_2\); the price indices).

Because \(M\) has non-zero columns only on that set \(S\), writing \(E_S\) for the coordinate selection gives \(M = M[\,\cdot\,,S]\,E_S^\top\), and the non-zero eigenvalues of a product \(AB\) coincide with those of \(BA\). Hence the non-zero spectrum of \(M\) is exactly the spectrum of the 588 \(\times\) 588 submatrix \(M[S,S]\), the remaining eigenvalues being zero. The whole spectrum is therefore computable.

One further property makes \(M\) interpretable across periods. \(M\) is time-varying, because the technology matrix drifts: \(A_i = (1-\phi_i)A_{i-1} + \phi_i B\), a partial adjustment toward the circular target \(B\). That recursion telescopes exactly to

\[A_i = B + (A_1 - B)\prod_{s \le i}(1 - \phi_s), \tag{A5.3}\]

verified against the implemented recursion to \(1.4 \times 10^{-17}\) against a target gap \(\max|B - A_1| = 0.0339\), with \(\phi\) constant within each purchasing region’s column block. The technology transition is thus a one-dimensional geometric mode per region: \(A\) slides along the fixed ray \((A_1 - B)\) toward the target. The time-variation in \(M\) that comes through technology is a scalar schedule known in advance, not an unknown drift. Two calibration facts complete the picture: the autonomous speed of adjustment is zero in both regions and government-expenditure growth is zero, so government spending is the sole driver of circular-economy technical change in this model, and with expenditure flat the adjustment rate is constant over the whole horizon (\(\phi^{Z_1} = 0.1182\) in every period).

The spectrum of the transition matrix

\(M\) was formed explicitly as a \(588\times588\) matrix by finite differences: each of the 588 lagged coordinates was perturbed alone by a relative \(\varepsilon = 10^{-6}\) and one period re-solved, the solve using a fixed 130 Gauss–Seidel sweeps rather than the adaptive stopping rule, because the adaptive rule’s iteration count is integer-valued and makes the period map discontinuous in \(s_{t-1}\). The restriction to 588 coordinates was itself verified rather than assumed: perturbing a sample of the excluded live variables moves the next period’s state by exactly zero.

\(M\) was built independently at three points on the baseline path, \(t = 30\), \(t = 50\) and \(t = 80\). All three give the same division of the spectrum into three parts:

so that \(\rho(M) = 1.02167305 > 1\) for the archived configuration. The two roots outside the unit circle are real; their eigenvectors load heavily on government bills and central-bank bill holdings. Numerically,

\[\lambda_1 = 1.02167305 = 1 + r_b^{Z_2}, \qquad \lambda_2 = 1.02159126 = 1 + r_b^{Z_1},\]

matching the calibrated bill rates \(r_b^{Z_2} = 0.0216730467\) and \(r_b^{Z_1} = 0.0215912638\) to within \(4\times10^{-9}\) — the resolution of the finite difference. The subsequent accounting audit found that one root is an economic mode through the unrecycled official-sector bill circuit, while the other lies off the bill-market identity and is an accounting-residue direction. The lack of a fiscal reaction function leaves the economic circuit undamped; the two roots do not establish two independent regional debt-compounding mechanisms.

The 105 unit roots are the stock-accumulation identities — cumulative CO\(_2\), the capital stocks, cumulative waste, durable consumption, central-bank holdings — each of which adds a flow to a stock with no depreciation or with depreciation netted elsewhere. Because 105 eigenvalues coincide, the individual eigenvectors within that cluster are not identified (any basis of the invariant subspace serves), so only the set of variable blocks it spans is reported and not the split between them.

\(\rho(M)\) is unchanged to eight decimal places across all three base points. That is not a coincidence and it does not indicate that \(M\) is time-invariant — \(M\) does vary, through the technology drift of (A5.3). It follows from the leading eigenvalue being \(1 + r_b\): the policy rate and its mark-up are both constant in the calibration, so the dominant root is a fixed parameter of the model rather than an outcome of it.

This spectrum is evidence about local dynamics in the archived configuration at the three measured base points. It does not establish attraction of a shocked trajectory, economic admissibility of its financial stocks, or stability of the current revised arm.

The damped cycle and the reporting window

The damped cycle and the government accelerator. A baseline with both accelerators on returns to rest through a damped cycle of about 22 periods: the firms’ (3.2), and the government’s, which closes the gap to a target public capital stock at speed \(\gamma_g\). In North/South \(\gamma_g = 0\), so public investment replaces depreciation and the cycle is damped away. The North/South fiat baseline turns 1 time(s) over \(t = 10\ldots100\); with \(\gamma_g = 0.15\) it would turn 8 times (R/dynamics/gamma_g_cycle.R). Lowering the firms’ speed of adjustment from 0.15 to 0.05 also removes the oscillation. This is the multiplier–accelerator interaction of Samuelson (1939). In the spectrum of the North/South gold comparator the cycle is one complex pair, loading on sectoral output and employment, the variables the capital target reads, and on net foreign liabilities: period 23.9, \(|\lambda|\) = 0.742, a half-life of 2.3 periods. The mode is present, but damped too fast to show as a cycle.

The reporting window. Because a baseline with both accelerators on (\(\gamma_g > 0\), as in the EU/RoW setup of earlier versions) is still cycling when the interventions start at \(t = 70\), a response read at a single date would depend on the phase of the cycle at that date. Every response in this paper is therefore the mean over \(t = 79\ldots100\), one full cycle, of the run’s deviation from its own unshocked baseline, and the cross-border classification is applied to that mean. It is a medium-run comparison, not a steady-state effect; Section 5 reports the drift after \(t = 100\).

D1 Cluster structure of the 48 runs

The clustering describes the signed responses of the 48 runs of the paper’s Results: North/South, OPENFIX-fiat, the North’s money as the reserve asset, the North making each of the twelve interventions under each of the four fiscal rules (no rule, the South adjusts, the North adjusts, both adjust). Data and method. Each run is described by its 74 responses (the variables of both regions, each a mean over \(t = 79\ldots100\) against the run’s own unshocked baseline). Each response is standardised across the 48 runs, and each of the six blocks (ecological 16, external 8, finance and wealth 10, fiscal 10, labour and distribution 18, real activity 12 responses) is given equal weight by dividing its columns by the square root of the block size. Runs are joined by Ward’s criterion on Euclidean distances: at each step the two groups whose union adds least to the within-group sum of squares are merged. The quality of a cut into \(k\) groups is read from the mean silhouette width, which compares each run’s distance to its own group with its distance to the nearest other group, on a scale from −1 to 1 on which values above 0.5 indicate reasonable structure.

A2 Alternative exchange-rate closures (removed)

Not in the paper. Removed on 2026-11-26 in the restructure into Parts A-D: the alternative exchange-rate closures (gold, OPENFIXG, quasi-float). The paper uses OPENFIX-fiat and its reversed hierarchy, both now in Block 10 of A1, which also keeps one paragraph on gold settlement for the paper's footnote. Shown as last rendered, at a771c74c.

An external closure specifies which variable absorbs an imbalance between the two areas: the stock of official reserves, government spending or the exchange rate. The production, price, income and portfolio equations (Blocks 0 to 9 and 11 to 13 of Appendix A1) are shared by all closures. Comparing closures therefore shows how settlement institutions modify the response of the two economies to the same circular-economy intervention.

Each alternative closure is defined by the equations it replaces in Block 10 (and, in one case, in Block 5), while every other equation is unchanged. Equations are numbered after the equation they replace, followed by a letter that identifies the closure: for instance, (10.7A) replaces equation (10.7) under closure A. In every case, the initial run of Block 0 is solved under the gold-settlement closure (A), and the selected closure applies from period 1 onwards. Each closure is calibrated and assessed against its own unshocked baseline.

Where settlement involves interest-bearing foreign bills, the model embodies an explicit monetary hierarchy: the central bank of one area holds the bills of the other area as reserves, whereas the reverse does not happen. Throughout, \(z\) denotes the reserve-holding area and \(f\) the area whose bills serve as reserves. The reserve-holding area is the European Union in the EU/RoW setup and the South in the North/South setup, so that the North/South equations are obtained by swapping the area indices. This assignment is a choice of closure, not a property of the regions. Under closures A and C, the foreign-bill holdings of both central banks are frozen, and gold settles the balance of payments, so that no area’s money plays the role of world money and the closure is neutral between the two areas. R. Fixed Exchange Rate with Fiat Reserves (OPENFIX-fiat)

This is the reference closure presented in Block 10 of Appendix A1, and it is recalled here for comparison. Following Godley and Lavoie (2007, sec. 12.4), interest-bearing bills of the other area replace gold as the moving reserve asset, which makes the monetary hierarchy and the associated interest-income circuit explicit. The exchange rate and the gold stocks are fixed, and the central bank of the reserve-holding area absorbs the residual supply of the other area’s bills:

\[ xr^{z} = xr_{- 1}^{z} \tag{10.0} \]

\[ B_{cb,z}^z = B_{cb,z,-1}^z + \Delta H_s^z - \Delta A_s^z - xr^f\,\Delta B_{cb,s,z}^f \tag{10.2} \]

\[ B_{cb,f}^f = B_{cb,f,-1}^f + \Delta H_s^f - \Delta A_s^f \tag{10.3} \]

\[ B_{s,z}^f = xr^z \cdot B_{h,z}^f \tag{10.4} \]

\[ B_{cb,s,z}^f = B_s^f - B_{s,z}^f - B_{s,f}^f - B_{cb,f}^f - B_b^f \tag{10.5} \]

\[ OR^z = OR_{-1}^z \tag{10.7} \]

\[ OR^f = OR_{-1}^f \tag{10.8} \]

where \(OR^{z}\) is the stock of gold held by the central bank of area \(z\). The redundant equation is the horizontal constraint on the market for the bills of the reserve-holding area:

\[ B_{cb,z}^z = B_s^z - B_{s,z}^z - B_{s,f}^z - B_b^z \tag{10.6} \]

Since reserves earn the interest rate of the issuing area, which accrues to the reserve-holding area as central-bank profit (5.9), a current account surplus increases reserves and their interest income, which further increases the surplus. Reserves therefore follow a recurrence of the form \(B_{cb,s,z,t + 1}^{f} = \left( 1 + r_{b,t}^{f} \right) \cdot B_{cb,s,z,t}^{f} + \Phi_{t}\), where \(\Phi_{t}\) is the non-interest component of the current account, expressed in foreign currency, and nothing in the model reacts to the stock of reserves. The baseline is therefore calibrated to a nil current account, so that reserves remain close to zero and the ratios of public debt to value added remain on target. This is a calibrated reference point rather than a general stability result: a shock that opens a current account imbalance may restart the compounding of reserves, so that a stationary baseline does not by itself establish a new stationary equilibrium after the shock.

A. Fixed Exchange Rate with Gold Settlement (OPENFIX-gold)

Motivation. Gold settlement provides the canonical fixed exchange rate reference (Godley and Lavoie, 2007, sec. 12.4) without allowing interest-bearing foreign reserves to create the compounding interest circuit described above. Gold earns no interest, so that the interest paid on each government’s bills remains within its own area. The settlement convention can therefore be compared with an explicitly hierarchical fiat-reserve system.

Equations. The exchange rate is fixed (10.0), and the supply of foreign bills to domestic households is unchanged (10.4). Each central bank is the residual holder of its own government’s bills:

\[ B_{cb,z}^z - B_{cb,z,-1}^z,\qquad B_{cb,z}^z = B_s^z - B_{s,z}^z - B_{s,f}^z - B_b^z \tag{10.2A} \]

\[ B_{cb,f}^f = B_s^f - B_{s,f}^f - B_{s,z}^f - B_b^f - B_{cb,s,z}^f \tag{10.3A} \]

while the stock of foreign bills held by the central bank of the reserve-holding area is frozen (it is nil in the base-year data):

\[ B_{cb,s,z}^f = B_{cb,s,z,-1}^f \tag{10.5A} \]

The balance of payments settles in gold. Each central bank’s stock of gold is the residual of its balance sheet:

\[ OR_{-1}^z + \frac{\Delta H_s^z - \Delta A_s^z - \Delta B_{cb,z}^z - \Delta(xr^f B_{cb,s,z}^f)}{p_{or}^z},\quad OR_{1}^z = \bar{G}^z \tag{10.7A} \]

\[ OR_{-1}^f + \frac{\Delta H_s^f - \Delta A_s^f - \Delta B_{cb,f}^f}{p_{or}^f},\quad OR_{1}^f = \bar{G}^f \tag{10.8A} \]

where \(p_{or}^{z}\) is the price of gold in the currency of area \(z\), and \({\bar{G}}^{z}\) is the initial gold endowment, which corresponds to the initial net worth of the central bank. The redundant equation, which is checked rather than computed, is the conservation of the world stock of gold:

\[ B_s^z - B_{s,z}^z - B_{s,f}^z - B_b^z\qquad(\text{assigned by 10.2; the redundant check is } \textstyle\sum_z p_{or}^z\,\Delta OR^z = 0) \tag{10.6A} \]

Adjustment. Gold stocks absorb the settlement flows, and their reallocation closes the external accounts, while production, income and fiscal balances respond through the rest of the model. Since nothing reacts to the stock of gold, changes in public debt and in reserve levels may persist after real activity has settled. Calibrating the baseline is a reference condition: it does not imply that stocks return to their original levels after a disturbance.

B. Fixed Reserves and Fiscal Adjustment (OPENFIXG)

Motivation. This closure asks how a fixed exchange rate regime adjusts when the reserve-holding area cannot absorb external imbalances by changing its stock of reserves. It makes the fiscal cost of the settlement constraint explicit. Following Godley and Lavoie’s OPENFIXG model (2007, chap. 12), the fiscal stance of the area that sits lower in the monetary hierarchy is made to target its external balance.

Equations. The exchange rate, the reserve stocks and the bill rates are fixed. The central bank of the reserve-holding area cannot buy bills beyond what its issue of money requires, so that its holding of its own government’s bills follows its balance sheet, and its gold stock is frozen:

\[ B_{cb,z}^z = B_{cb,z,-1}^z + \Delta H_s^z - \Delta A_s^z \tag{10.2B} \]

\[ OR^z = OR_{-1}^z \tag{10.7B} \]

The supply of bills of the reserve-holding area is whatever households, banks, the other area and the central bank demand at the fixed interest rate. The government deficit is the change in that stock, and real government spending becomes the residual of the budget constraint, replacing the exogenous rule (5.1):

\[ B_s^z\;\;(\texttt{b\_s}) = B_{s,z}^z + B_{s,f}^z + B_b^z + B_{cb,z}^z \tag{5.6aB} \]

\[ DEF_g^z\;\;(\texttt{gdef}) = B_s^z - B_{s,-1}^z \tag{5.5B} \]

\[ gov^z\;\;(\texttt{g}) = \big(DEF_g^z - r_{b,-1}^z B_{s,-1}^z + F_{cb}^z + T^z + VAT^z + TAR^z - i_{d,g}^z p_{I,g}^z\big) / p_G^z \tag{5.1B} \]

The other area keeps its exogenous spending rule (5.1), and the remaining equations of Block 10 are those of closure A, namely (10.3A), (10.5A) and (10.8A). Since the gold stock of the reserve-holding area is frozen, the redundant equation states that the gold stock of the other area does not change either:

\[ \Delta OR^f = 0 \tag{10.6B} \]

Adjustment. The government of the reserve-holding area can borrow only what wealth holders choose to absorb. A current account deficit drains private income and wealth, lowers the demand for bills, reduces the admissible deficit and therefore cuts government spending; a surplus works the other way round. This is the twin-deficits logic of Godley and Lavoie’s OPENFIXG model. It is not a debt-reduction rule, and it implies austerity only when the external position worsens. A symmetric assignment of reserves has no adjusting area and is therefore not an admissible setting for this closure.

C. Crawling Exchange Rate with Gold Settlement (Quasi-Float)

Motivation. The quasi-float allows external prices to adjust gradually, while retaining a separate channel for the settlement of reserves. Its lagged rule avoids making the exchange rate a variable that clears the bill market within the period.

Equations. The exchange rate of the first area crawls with its lagged current account, expressed as a share of net income, replacing the fixed rate (10.0):

\[ xr^z = xr_{-1}^z + par_{xr}\, \frac{CAB_{-1}^z}{YN_{-1}^z} \tag{10.0C} \]

where \(\chi_{xr} > 0\) is the speed of the crawl, and \(z\) denotes the first area (\(Z1\)), since the closure is neutral between the two areas. The reciprocal rate is given by (10.1), and gold settles the remaining imbalance, as in closure A: equations (10.2A), (10.3A), (10.5A), (10.7A), (10.8A) and the redundant equation (10.6A) apply.

Adjustment. A current account surplus of the first area raises its exchange rate in the following period. The change in buyer prices alters the Armington substitution between origins and, hence, trade flows, while gold settles the residual imbalance. The substitution between origins of intermediate inputs (13.3) is therefore essential: without it, an appreciation raises the trade balance of the appreciating area, and the exchange rate diverges. All the terms of the rule are lagged, so that there is no simultaneous loop. However, this is a numerical property rather than a proof of dynamic stability after a disturbance.

Dynamical analysis of the fiat reference

The model is a system of 6,747 difference equations, one per state row, of which 2,976 move during a run. Every lag is exactly one period, so it is a first-order system, \(\mathbf s_t = F(\mathbf s_t, \mathbf s_{t-1})\), implicit in \(\mathbf s_t\) because the period is solved by Gauss–Seidel iteration. Two questions organise its structure.

Which equations are simultaneous: the dependency graph and its core. Write an edge \(j \to i\) when equation \(i\) reads the current value of \(j\). Of the graph’s 1,949 strongly connected components (maximal sets of variables each reaching every other), 1,944 are single equations evaluated in sequence; one has 1,024 variables, the simultaneous core, identical in both setups. It contains, for each region, the 54-sector vectors of final demand, output, prices, employment, value added and incomes, with consumption, investment, replacement, the capital stock, imports and tax revenues: the circular flow of the surplus approach, written as the Keynesian multiplier through the input–output system. This is the graph-theoretic form of Sraffa’s distinction between basic and non-basic commodities. Joining the lagged reads shows that no ecological or distributional indicator returns to the core in any number of periods (Supplementary Appendix B3): emissions, extraction, land, water and the income shares are non-basic; ecological damage is accounted, not transmitted.

How the system moves between periods: linearisation. Linearising about the baseline path, \(d\mathbf s_t = J_c\,d\mathbf s_t + J_\ell\,d\mathbf s_{t-1}\), so \(d\mathbf s_t = M\,d\mathbf s_{t-1}\) with \(M = (I - J_c)^{-1}J_\ell\): a within-period multiplier, a Leontief inverse across equations, times the lagged reads, whose 1,076 non-zero columns carry all the dynamics. \(M\) is computed by finite differences of the full period solve (Supplementary Appendix B4). A complex pair of its eigenvalues is a damped oscillation with period \(2\pi/\arg\lambda\) and damping \(|\lambda|\) per period, and a unit root a stock that accumulates a flow.

The full transition spectrum was calculated for the gold-closure comparator. It has not been calculated for the fiat reference.

The damped cycle and the government accelerator. A baseline with both accelerators on returns to rest through a damped cycle of about 22 periods: the firms’ (3.2), and the government’s, which closes the gap to a target public capital stock at speed \(\gamma_g\). In North/South \(\gamma_g = 0\), so public investment replaces depreciation and the cycle is damped away. The North/South fiat baseline turns 1 time(s) over \(t = 10\ldots100\); with \(\gamma_g = 0.15\) it would turn 8 times (R/dynamics/gamma_g_cycle.R). Lowering the firms’ speed of adjustment from 0.15 to 0.05 also removes the oscillation. This is the multiplier–accelerator interaction of Samuelson (1939). In the spectrum of the North/South gold comparator the cycle is one complex pair, loading on sectoral output and employment, the variables the capital target reads, and on net foreign liabilities: period 23.9, \(|\lambda|\) = 0.742, a half-life of 2.3 periods. The mode is present, but damped too fast to show as a cycle.

The reporting window. Because a baseline with both accelerators on (\(\gamma_g > 0\), as in the EU/RoW setup of earlier versions) is still cycling when the interventions start at \(t = 70\), a response read at a single date would depend on the phase of the cycle at that date. Every response in this paper is therefore the mean over \(t = 79\ldots100\), one full cycle, of the run’s deviation from its own unshocked baseline, and the cross-border classification is applied to that mean. It is a medium-run comparison, not a steady-state effect; Section 5 reports the drift after \(t = 100\).

Calibration detail

Data and units. The model is built on EXIOBASE 3.8.2 (Stadler et al., 2021, 2018) for 2011. Its 44 countries and five rest-of-world blocks are aggregated to two regions, and its 163 industries to 54 per region, through explicit concordances (Supplementary Appendix B1) into the Global North and the Global South. Taken from the data without fitting are the inter-industry coefficients by supplying and using region, the capital-flow matrix \(K\), the target capital–output ratios, labour productivity and employment, the final-demand share vectors by channel and origin, the emission, material, land and water intensities, and the 2011 aggregates used as targets. One model unit is EUR 10 bn; gross output and final demand are volumes at 2011 basic prices, stocks and income flows nominal, and material throughput is in units of 10 Mt (EXIOBASE used plus unused extraction). The money wage is anchored at its 2011 employment-weighted mean, and \(e\) in \(r = r_b + e\) reproduces the observed 2011 price level.

Stocks, free parameters and stationarity. The input–output data give flows but no stocks, so every financial and real stock starts at zero; the model is run for 60 unshocked periods from 2011 flows, and every experiment restarts from the state that run reaches, with interventions at \(t = 70\) of the main run. The parameters the data do not give are set by Newton searches over a few named instruments — the two government-debt endowments, the intercepts \(\lambda^{E,0}\) of the equity shares of household wealth, and scale factors on the two income-tax rates — against targets at \(t = 100\): government debt of 0.98 (North) and 0.36 (South) of value added (IMF WEO, 2011), a zero current account, firm loans of 1.2 (North) and 0.81 (South) of value added, and value added at its stationary 2011 level. A point is accepted only if the baseline is stationary and on target by the tests of Supplementary Appendix B2. The accepted North/South fiat baseline has firm loans of 1.209 (North) and 0.824 (South) of value added, government debt of 0.976 and 0.363, debt-ratio slopes of at most 4e-05 per period and \(|\text{CAB}/\text{VA}|\) of 4e-05. Coefficients with no 2011 counterpart are fixed by one-parameter scans, each with a criterion stated before its result: the Armington elasticity on intermediate inputs (\(\varepsilon_{int} = 1.0\), the smallest value at which a depreciation improves the trade balance in every run), the portfolio scale (\(\sigma = 4\), the largest value that passed the acceptance tests), and the finance and credit coefficients. None of these has an empirical source. Supplementary Appendix B2 gives the searches, parameters and initial conditions.

The stress setting. The Results run on a stress calibration. The North’s firm-loan target is 1.20 of value added, above what the 2011 data imply; the finance coefficients (margin and leverage in the capital target, the liability premium) are five times their calibrated values, retention is 0.2, and the credit-rationing coefficients are raised to 0.5 and 1, so that the finance constraint and credit rationing have a balance sheet to act on. These are not estimates. On the EU/RoW grid of an earlier version, where this was measured, the finance terms and their interaction with the scenarios accounted for 15.9% of the standardised variation of the scenario responses, against 3.2% at the calibrated values.

Fit to the 2011 targets. In a nearly stationary baseline, \(k_t \simeq k_{t-1}\) implies \(i_d \simeq da\): most gross private investment replaces capital used up, while net capital accumulation is small. That is expected from the baseline design, but limits its fit to the positive net investment of a growing 2011 economy. The fit criterion, the sum of squared deviations from target in units of each region’s value added, is 0.00839 for the North/South fiat baseline. The North is within 5% of target on every concept except its trade flows; the main misses are the South’s investment, value added and trade (Supplementary Appendix B2 gives the full table).

The reported baseline. Every scenario is measured against the unshocked baseline of its own arm. The four arms of the 48 runs share one baseline, since a fiscal rule acts only on the gap from the unshocked path. North value added in that baseline moves within a range of 0.007% over \(t = 70\ldots100\) and has 1 turning point(s) over \(t = 10\ldots100\).
